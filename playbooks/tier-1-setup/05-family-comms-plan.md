# Family Communications Plan

The one-page document that determines whether your household regroups in two hours or two days.

## If you only read one line

**Pick one person outside your area to be the hub. When phones are working but local circuits are jammed, long-distance often still goes through.** Everyone calls the hub. The hub tracks status.

## Why a plan matters

In a disaster, the mistakes people make are predictable:

- They try the same number repeatedly and get a fast busy signal.
- They text a group chat, watch nothing happen, and panic.
- They drive to find family, adding traffic and risk.
- They assume silence means the worst.

A plan converts all of this into: "call the hub, leave a message, proceed to the meeting point."

### What each person does

**In a sudden emergency (earthquake, fire alarm, violence nearby):**

- Everyone: get to safety immediately. Do not wait to coordinate.
- When safe: call the hub. State "I'm okay" or "I'm hurt, at [location]."
- Proceed to the primary meeting point if you cannot return home.

**In a slow-moving emergency (hurricane warning, pandemic declaration, approaching wildfire):**

- Adults: check in within 30 minutes, decide to stay or go within 2 hours, execute plan.
- Kids at school: school will either shelter or release — follow school plan, parent with designated pickup picks them up.
- Work: notify household of plans and location before leaving work.

### Code phrases (optional but useful)

Simple phrases that mean something specific without explaining on the phone:

- "**Execute the plan**" = initiate evacuation to primary destination now.
- "**Need the red folder**" = grab the emergency documents packet and get out.
- "**I need a ride home**" = I am not okay / feel unsafe / want an excuse to leave.

Write these down for your household. Practice using them so they feel normal.

### Out-of-area contact's job

Your hub holds:

- A copy of the household roster (if they agree).
- A list of who should be checking in (everyone in the household).
- Permission to relay messages between household members if local circuits are down.
- Discretion about what to share publicly (no social media posts unless authorized).

## Communication methods, by scenario

### Phones working normally

- SMS is more resilient than voice during network congestion.
- Long-distance often routes when local doesn't.
- Group texts preserve history for everyone; use one.
- Don't tie up lines: state-check-end. "I'm okay, at home, will call when I move. Love you."

### Cell voice down, data up

- iMessage / SMS often still work.
- Signal, WhatsApp work over data.
- Email works.
- Social media status posts (to a private circle) let many people know at once.

### Cell down entirely

- WiFi calling works if any router + internet is reachable (coffee shop, neighbor's router if you know the password).
- Landlines (if anyone still has one) often survive cell outages.
- Ham / GMRS radio: if anyone in the household has one. See [`../cards/radio-frequencies.md`](../cards/radio-frequencies.md).
- Go to the primary meeting point. Show up in person.

### Power and communications down

- Runner: adult with a bike or on foot between meeting points at pre-agreed intervals.
- Paper note on the door of your home: "We went to [meeting point]. Date/time. [Name]."
- Signalling: see [`../frameworks/signalling-and-rescue.md`](../frameworks/signalling-and-rescue.md).

### Different time zones

The hub's role becomes critical if your household splits geographically. Agree in advance on:

- The time window when the hub expects calls (e.g., "I'll be awake 7 AM–11 PM Pacific for as long as needed").
- How they communicate with you if it's the middle of the night for you (text, not call).

## Kids

- **Teach your kids the out-of-area contact's phone number by age 5 if possible.**
- Teach them *your* phone number. Most kids don't know it because they dial by name.
- Put an ID card in their backpack with parent names, phones, medical info, allergies.
- Each kid has a small object in their go-bag with their name, parent names, and the out-of-area contact's number on it.
- Teach them what "the plan" means before they need to use it. Rehearse as a calm conversation, not a drill that scares them.
- If separated: kids go to a trusted adult (teacher, neighbor, officer in uniform) and say "I need to call my mom/dad. This number: XXX-XXX-XXXX."

## Schools and workplaces

- **Know your child's school's emergency plan.** Who releases the child. Where reunification happens. What phone number is the school emergency line.
- **Who can pick up**: every school has a pickup authorization list. Update it.
- **Your workplace**: know the evacuation plan, shelter plan, and whether you'll be expected to stay during a local emergency (some hospitals and utilities expect staff to shelter in place on-site).

## Pets

- Decide in advance: primary hosts, will they accept pets?
- If not, boarding backup — a friend or kennel willing to take them in a crisis.
- Pets in evacuation: leash + carrier + food + water + meds + vaccination records + familiar blanket.

## Testing

Once every 6 months:

- **Execute a light drill.** Text everyone: "This is a test. Call the out-of-area contact, confirm, call me back." See how long it takes. Find the weak link.
- **Update the hub** if their circumstances change (move, phone number, new baby, changed job).
- **Talk through a scenario at dinner.** "What if the power went out tonight and stayed out for a week?"

## Privacy

Do not commit this file to git with real names and numbers. Save your working copy as `family-comms-private.md` (gitignored) or offline. The file you *do* commit should be this template, blank.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. Pick your out-of-area contact. Call them. Ask if they'll do it.
2. Fill in the form above. Print one copy per household member.
3. Each member puts theirs in their wallet + go-bag + backpack.
4. Tell kids what "the plan" means and who the hub is.
5. Put a calendar reminder for 6 months out to test.
