# Water & Food Stockpile

The single cheapest, highest-return prep. A 14-day store is the threshold where most short-to-medium crises become routine instead of lethal.

## If you only read one line

**Store what you actually eat. Rotate through it.** A pile of survival rations you'll never touch is worse than a working pantry you refresh naturally.

## The targets

- **Water**: 1 gallon per person per day × 14 days = **14 gallons/person minimum**. Doubling this is reasonable for hot climates or households with pets.
- **Food**: 2,000 kcal/person/day × 14 days = **28,000 kcal/person minimum**.
- **Cooking fuel**: enough to heat food and purify water for the full period if the utilities are out.

Fourteen days is the line below which "stocking up" becomes "having what you need for a normal power outage or water main break." Above that, you're in proper disaster-prep territory and returns start to diminish (pests, rotation, spoilage).

Citation: FEMA and American Red Cross recommend 3-day minimums. The 14-day target matches ongoing guidance from Washington State Emergency Management and most major-metro resilience planners, which is more realistic for Cascadia earthquake and multi-day storm scenarios.

## Water

### How much, where

- 1 gallon/person/day covers drinking + minimal hygiene.
- For infants, elderly, pregnant/nursing, sick: add 50%.
- For pets: ~1 oz per pound of body weight per day.
- Store cool, out of direct sunlight, off concrete floors (can leach).

### Options by price

| Option | Cost (family of 4, 2 weeks) | Pros | Cons |
|--------|------------------------------|------|------|
| Store-bought 1-gallon jugs | ~$30–60 | Easy, sealed, works | Takes shelf space |
| Reused 2-liter soda bottles, tap water | Free | Free, recycles | Rotate every 6 months; won't fit all needs |
| 5-gallon water bricks | ~$100 one-time | Stackable, durable | Upfront cost |
| 55-gallon water barrel | ~$80 + treatment | Large volume | Heavy, hard to move, needs pump |
| Bathtub + WaterBOB | ~$30 for the liner | 60+ gallons when you need it most | Only works if you have advance notice |

Do not store tap water in milk jugs — they degrade and leak. Clean 2-liter soda bottles work well.

### Treatment and rotation

- Unopened commercial water: indefinite, practically ~2 years before plastic taste dominates.
- Tap water in clean containers: rotate every 6 months. Label the fill date.
- If water looks or smells off, treat before drinking: boil 1 minute at sea level (3 min above 6,500 ft), OR 2 drops unscented household bleach per liter, wait 30 min.
- Filter-then-treat is the gold standard for questionable water. Sawyer Squeeze or MSR Guardian + bleach is overkill for most scenarios and correct for all of them.

Citation: `survival-guides/Emergency_Water_Purification_Guide.pdf`, `survival-guides/Water_Purification_Methods.pdf`.

### Collecting water if stores run out

- Water heater tanks: 30–50 gallons of potable water. Shut off cold inlet, open lowest drain valve (turn off power/gas to the heater first).
- Toilet tank (the *back*, not the bowl): potable if no chemical cleaners are used in it.
- Rainwater: collect off clean roofing; filter and treat before drinking.
- Nearby creeks/ponds: filter to 0.2 microns AND treat. Assume contamination from upstream sources.

Water from pools, hot tubs, dehumidifiers, or water beds is for sanitation, not drinking.

## Food

### What to buy (for a normal-eating household)

Buy foods you already eat. "Survival food" you don't like gets skipped in the hungry moment you packed it for.

**Calorie-dense staples (these do the heavy lifting):**

- Rice (white, brown, or both). 25 lb bag is ~38,000 kcal, ~$20.
- Dry beans / lentils. 10 lb = ~16,000 kcal.
- Oats. 5 lb = ~9,000 kcal.
- Pasta. 4 lb = ~6,000 kcal.
- Flour. 5 lb, if you know how to use it. Without a leavener or oven, limited.
- Cooking oil. 1 gallon = ~32,000 kcal. The cheapest calorie density in a pantry.
- Sugar / honey. Stores indefinitely; calorie source; barter-friendly.
- Salt. Non-negotiable. At least 2 lb/person.

**Protein and fat:**

- Canned fish (sardines, mackerel, tuna). Shelf life 3+ years.
- Canned meat (chicken, beef). 2+ years.
- Peanut butter. 2 years.
- Powdered eggs or cheese (optional, longer-term).
- Nuts in sealed packaging.

**Convenience and morale:**

- Instant coffee, tea bags, bouillon cubes, hot cocoa.
- Shelf-stable milk (UHT) for kids.
- Hard candy for kids and long stressful days.
- Familiar snacks (crackers, granola bars). Morale is calories in a crisis.

**Cooking adjuncts:**

- Spices you actually use.
- Vinegar, soy sauce, or similar acids.
- Bouillon (salt + flavor in one).
- Baking soda (cooking, cleaning, fire extinguishing, oral hygiene backup).

**What to skip (mostly):**

- Bulk MRE cases. Expensive, heavy, shelf life shorter than advertised.
- 25-year freeze-dried buckets. Fine if you have money to burn, but a rotating pantry is better.
- "Prepper" canned bread or emergency wafers. Almost always bad food.

### Storage

- Cool, dark, dry, pest-proof.
- Mylar bags + oxygen absorbers + food-grade 5-gallon buckets extend most staples to 20+ years. Overkill for 14-day targets; start thinking about it if you extend to 3+ month storage.
- Keep an inventory list. A few lines on a piece of paper taped inside the pantry door is sufficient.

### Rotation

The pantry model: when you open a new can, replace it on the next grocery run. This keeps everything fresh, uses your normal eating to sustain the store, and doesn't require a big annual rotation event.

### Cooking without power

Assume your stove, microwave, and oven are out.

- **Fuel options**: camp stove (butane, propane, white gas), charcoal grill (**outdoors only** — CO kills in enclosed spaces), rocket stove, kelly kettle, fireplace.
- **Rule of thumb**: 1 small canister of butane or 1 propane bottle covers ~14 days of modest cooking for a small household.
- **No-cook default**: peanut butter, jerky, canned fish, bread, bars — can be eaten without any heat. Useful for the first 48 hours when you may not have set up cooking yet.

**Never cook indoors without ventilation.** Charcoal and gasoline generators indoors have killed more people than the disasters they were prepping for.

## Food safety in a power outage

### Fridge and freezer

- Refrigerator (unopened): safe for ~4 hours without power.
- Full freezer: safe for ~48 hours unopened. Half-full: ~24 hours.
- Add bags of ice or frozen water jugs if you have warning.
- Throw out any food above 40°F for more than 2 hours (cumulative). When in doubt, discard.

### Canning safety

Home-canned food is a real food source but not casual. Bacterial growth in improperly canned food can kill — botulism is the actual risk. Follow the USDA guide in `food-water/USDA_Complete_Guide_Home_Canning_2015.pdf`. The summary: pressure-can low-acid foods (meat, vegetables), water-bath-can only high-acid foods (most fruits, pickled things). If a jar seal is broken, lid pops, or contents smell off — throw it out, do not taste.

## Infant and special needs

- **Infants**: formula (powder keeps longer than liquid concentrate), bottles, sterilizing tabs, plenty of water.
- **Low-sodium / diabetic / celiac diets**: stock what *they* eat, not the generic list.
- **Elderly**: softer foods if dental issues; higher protein needs.
- **Allergies**: double-check canned goods for cross-contamination warnings.

## Morale food

A 14-day indoors stretch gets grim fast on rice and beans. Include:

- One thing per family member they genuinely like.
- Chocolate, hard candy, or a comfort drink.
- Something to celebrate with if a milestone passes (end of week one, kid's birthday, etc.).

Food is not just calories; in crisis, it's one of the few sources of pleasure left. Don't ration yourself into despair.

A weekend of purposeful grocery runs, not a major expense.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. This week's grocery run: add 1 item per category from the staple list. Done that 6 weeks in a row, you have most of a 14-day store.
2. Fill and label 4 × 2-liter bottles with tap water. Put them in the back of a closet. Rotate every 6 months.
3. Check your water heater: know where the drain valve is, know how to shut off the inlet. Practice once.
4. Buy a cheap camp stove and one canister of fuel. Use it on a normal camping trip before an emergency.
