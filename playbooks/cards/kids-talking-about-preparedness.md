# Talking To Kids About Preparedness

**If you only read one line: name the fear, show the helper, then show the family plan.**

## 1. Use teachable moments

**Start when the door is already open.**
- After a thunderstorm, talk about flashlights.
- After a school fire drill, walk the home exits.
- After a news event they already saw, ask what they heard.
- Keep it short. Stop while they still feel steady.

**Do not force a topic.**
- Do not start disaster talk at bedtime.
- Do not use graphic footage as teaching material.
- Do not rehearse danger without rehearsing the action.

## 2. Lead with helpers

**Name the people doing the work.**
- Firefighters.
- Nurses.
- Line crews.
- Road crews.
- Neighbors.
- Family.

**Use the helper frame.**
- "When something bad happens, people help."
- "Our family has helpers too."
- "Here is who we call. Here is where we meet."

## 3. Validate, then reassure

**Say the fear first.**
- "That looked scary."
- "You are worried it could happen here."
- "It makes sense that you want to know the plan."

**Then give the plan.**
- "If the smoke alarm sounds, we go to the mailbox."
- "If the lights go out, we get the flashlight box."
- "If phones are busy, we text Aunt Maya."

## 4. Turn "what if" into "we will"

**Make it concrete.**
- "What if I can't find you?" becomes "We will meet at the library steps."
- "What if the house shakes?" becomes "We will drop, cover, and hold on."
- "What if the power goes out?" becomes "We will eat the no-cook dinner and use the radio."

**Use the same words every time.**
- Same meeting spots.
- Same phone number.
- Same pickup people.
- Same job for the child.

## 5. Age-appropriate detail

**Preschool.**
- Concrete only.
- "The wind knocked a branch onto the wire."
- "Workers are fixing it."
- "We are using flashlights tonight."

**Elementary.**
- Simple cause and action.
- Let them pack one item.
- Let them practice the meeting spot.
- Correct wrong rumors fast.

**Tween.**
- Give more facts.
- Give a real role.
- Let them ask hard questions.
- Do not pretend uncertainty is certainty.

## 6. Limit media exposure

**Stop the replay loop.**
- Repeated footage feels like repeated danger.
- Turn off live coverage around young children.
- If an older child watches, watch with them.
- Answer questions; do not add graphic detail.

**Use official alerts, not doomscrolling.**
- Local emergency management.
- National Weather Service.
- School district.
- Fire or police public information feed.

## 7. Watch for delayed reactions

**Expect normal stress.**
- Clinginess.
- Bed-wetting.
- Nightmares.
- Irritability.
- Quiet withdrawal.
- Replaying the event in play.

**Keep routine.**
- Meals.
- Sleep.
- Schoolwork when school exists.
- Light chores.
- Comfort objects.

**Get help when it does not settle.**
- Talk to a pediatrician, school counselor, or child mental-health clinician if fear, anger, sadness, or sleep problems last more than 2 to 4 weeks, get worse, or disrupt school and relationships.

*Source: American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren.org "Talking With Children About Disasters"; CDC "Helping Children Cope with a Disaster"; American Red Cross "Building Resilience in Children" and "How Families Can Prepare for Emergencies"; SAMHSA children and disasters guidance; Fred Rogers Productions "look for the helpers" guidance.*
