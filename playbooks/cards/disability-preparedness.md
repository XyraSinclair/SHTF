# Disability Preparedness

**If you only read one line: protect the function you cannot lose: power, mobility, communication, medication, caregiver access.**

## 1. Universal principles

**Build around function.**
- List what must keep working: breathing, moving, hearing alerts, seeing labels, communicating, regulating sensory input, taking medicine.
- Aim for 14 days at home when specialized meds, supplies, or equipment are involved.
- Treat power as a medical need when a device, charger, fridge, bed, lift, or pump depends on it.
- Use redundant alerts: phone, radio, text, visual signal, bed-shaker, caregiver call.

**Put needs in writing.**
- Diagnosis and access needs.
- Medication list.
- Equipment list.
- Device settings.
- Supplier numbers.
- Emergency contacts.

## 2. Mobility impairment

**Plan for stairs and dead elevators.**
- Assign an evacuation partner.
- Know the area of refuge or stairwell plan.
- Keep a lightweight transfer sheet or evacuation sling if appropriate.
- Label chair, walker, cane, braces, charger, and spare parts.

**Back up the device.**
- Manual chair backup if you use a power chair.
- Patch kit, pump, wrench, charger, and spare battery where applicable.
- Keep paths wide enough for the device you actually use.

## 3. Blind / low-vision

**Make gear findable by touch.**
- Tactile labels on meds, water, food, batteries, and chargers.
- Rubber bands, raised dots, tape shapes, or braille labels.
- Same storage spot every time.

**Keep alerts audible.**
- Weather radio with tone alert.
- Phone alerts with sound and vibration.
- Extra cane tips.
- Service animal food, water, bowl, harness, leash, waste bags, and records.

**Brief the shelter.**
- Tell intake staff how you orient.
- Ask for clear paths.
- Ask staff to announce changes out loud, not only on signs.

## 4. Deaf / hard-of-hearing

**Make warnings visible and tactile.**
- Weather radio with text display, strobe, or bed-shaker.
- Text alerts from local emergency management.
- Visual smoke and CO alarms.
- Extra hearing-aid batteries or charger.

**Carry communication-access cards.**
- "I am deaf / hard of hearing."
- "Write it down."
- "Face me when speaking."
- "I need ASL interpretation."
- "Do not separate me from my device or interpreter."

## 5. Cognitive impairment

**Make the plan visible.**
- Picture-based checklist.
- Color-coded bags.
- One-step labels: "take this bag," "go to mailbox," "call Sam."
- Rehearse one route at a time.

**Reduce wandering risk.**
- Medical ID bracelet.
- Recent photo.
- Caregiver contact card in clothing, wallet, and bag.
- MedicAlert or similar emergency ID service if the household can maintain it.

## 6. Autism / sensory

**Pack regulation gear.**
- Noise-cancelling headphones.
- Sunglasses or brimmed hat.
- Stim or fidget items.
- Familiar object.
- Preferred shelf-stable foods.
- Comfort clothing without scratchy seams.

**Show the sequence.**
- Visual schedule.
- First/then card.
- Shelter script: "We will wait here. Then we will sleep. Then we will check the plan."
- Accommodations request letter from clinician, school, or caregiver when useful.

## 7. Registry and rights

**Register early.**
- Utility medical-baseline or life-support list.
- Local office of emergency management vulnerable-persons registry.
- Paratransit or accessible-ride registry.
- Supplier emergency number for oxygen, dialysis, wheelchair, bed, feeding pump, or communication device.

**Know the shelter rules.**
- ADA-covered emergency shelters must provide equal access and reasonable modifications.
- Service animals go where the public can go, including emergency shelters, unless a narrow safety exception applies.
- Mobility devices stay with the person unless there is a direct safety reason and an equal-access replacement.
- Essential caregivers, interpreters, and support workers are access needs, not convenience requests.
