# Psychological First Aid

**Panic, freeze, and grief kill plans as surely as bleeding kills bodies. You do not need a degree to help — you need presence, calm, and a framework.**

## Core principle — Look, Listen, Link

WHO's three actions. Memorize them.

- **Look.** Scan for safety, for obvious injuries, for who is overwhelmed. Who is quiet and shut down? Who is screaming? Who is bleeding? Who is alone?
- **Listen.** Approach. Introduce yourself. Ask what they need. Do not interrogate. Silence is a valid answer.
- **Link.** Connect them to basic needs (water, shelter, a blanket, information), to loved ones, and to further help when available.

## Before you help anyone

- Scene safe. Physical first — you cannot help a drowning person from the water.
- Your own oxygen mask: one slow breath, in 4, out 6. Ground yourself first.
- Ask permission before touching or moving someone. "Can I sit with you?" "Can I put this blanket on you?"
- You are not there to fix feelings. You are there to keep them safe and not alone.

## For the adult frozen in panic

- Name the state out loud, calmly: "You're in shock. That's normal. Breathe with me."
- **5-4-3-2-1 grounding:** name 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you feel, 2 you smell, 1 you taste.
- **Box breathing:** 4 in, 4 hold, 4 out, 4 hold. Do it with them, out loud.
- Assign a small task. "Hold this. Watch the door. Count the kids." Action breaks freeze.
- Do not argue. Do not rush. Do not say "calm down." It has never worked in the history of human speech.

## For a child

- Get to their eye level. Lower your voice, slow it down.
- Stay physically close. Hold their hand if welcome.
- Use short sentences. State what IS true. "You are with me. We are in this room. This is safe."
- Honesty, age-appropriate: "Something bad happened. We are okay right now. I am making a plan."
- Do NOT promise "everything will be fine." They stop trusting you the first time something isn't.
- Routine anchors: eat, drink, rest, story, small job.
- Validate without amplifying: "You're scared. Me too. We'll get through this together."

## For a teen

- Treat like an adult in information, like a child in reassurance.
- Give them a real role — carry the pack, watch the sibling, track the radio, manage the water.
- Screens may be lost. Acknowledge the grief of losing their social world; it is real to them.
- Privacy matters. Let them cry alone if they need to. Check on them.

## For grief and loss

- Presence, not solutions. Sit with them.
- Say the name of the person or thing lost. Silence around it hurts more than hearing it.
- Do NOT say: "at least…", "everything happens for a reason," "they're in a better place," "you're lucky it wasn't worse," "I know how you feel," "you should…"
- DO say: "I'm here." "This is terrible." "Tell me about them." "What do you need right now — company, quiet, a task?"

## Acute stress reactions in the first hours

Normal reactions. Not a disorder. Do not medicalize them.

- Shaking, nausea, crying, laughing inappropriately.
- Tunnel vision, time distortion, emotional numbness.
- Racing thoughts or going blank.
- Restless pacing or immobility.

These pass with warmth, safety, hydration, a blanket, and time. Sit near. Offer water. Do not demand they talk.

## Red flags — this person needs more than PFA

- Cannot be oriented to time, place, or person for over an hour.
- Repeatedly stating intent to harm self or others.
- Severe dissociation lasting hours, not responsive to gentle grounding.
- Cannot eat, drink, or sleep for over 24 hours.
- Hallucinations not tied to grief (not "I saw Grandma" — actual sensory breaks).

Stay with them. Do not leave them alone. Get to professional help as soon as the environment allows. **988** (US Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) works by call or text when cell or internet is up.

## For YOU — keeping the adult in charge functional

- Eat. Drink water. Sit for 10 minutes every 2 hours. You are no use unconscious.
- Pick the next **one** decision, not the whole plan.
- Delegate. Even a child can hold something.
- Write things down. Your memory is worse than you think right now.
- Cry when alone if you need to. Do not suppress it permanently — suppressed stress surfaces as bad decisions.

## Days and weeks later — delayed reactions

Normal for anyone who went through something hard:

- Nightmares, intrusive memories.
- Hypervigilance, exaggerated startle.
- Avoidance of reminders.
- Irritability, guilt ("why didn't I…"), numbness.
- Sleep disruption, appetite changes.

Expected. Usually settles over 2–4 weeks with safety, sleep, connection, and routine.

If symptoms persist past ~4 weeks, worsen, or wreck daily function, this may be acute stress disorder trending toward PTSD. Reach out for care. **Early intervention works.**

## What NOT to say, ever

- "It could have been worse."
- "At least no one died." (If someone did, it's monstrous. If not, it's dismissive.)
- "You should be over this by now."
- "Be strong for the kids / your spouse / your parents."
- "God doesn't give you more than you can handle."
- "Everything happens for a reason."

## What to say instead

- "I'm sorry this is happening."
- "I'm here. I'm not going anywhere."
- "You don't have to talk."
- "What do you need right now?"
- "I don't know what to say, but I'm staying with you."

## The minimum viable intervention

If you remember nothing else:

1. Get them safe.
2. Get them warm.
3. Get them water.
4. Sit beside them.
5. Do not say anything stupid.

That is 80% of psychological first aid.
