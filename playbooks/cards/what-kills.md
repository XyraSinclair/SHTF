# What Actually Kills People in Disasters

**The disaster gets the headlines. These fill the morgue. Most deaths happen in the hours, days, and weeks after the event — to people who survived the initial impact and then died from something preventable.**

## 1. Carbon monoxide poisoning

Leading secondary killer in hurricanes, ice storms, and any grid-down event. Colorless, odorless. Victims fall unconscious before they know.

- Generators, grills, camp stoves, pressure washers **outside only**, **20+ feet** from any door, window, or vent.
- Never run a generator in a garage — even with the door open.
- Never use a gas oven, charcoal, or propane heater indoors for warmth.
- Install **battery-powered CO alarms** on every level and outside sleeping areas. CO alarms are not smoke alarms. Buy both.
- Symptoms: headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion. Get everyone outside immediately, call 911.
- Do not re-enter until vented and tested.

## 2. Heat stroke

Largest US weather-related killer most years. Kills elderly who shut off AC to save money, outdoor workers, and people in vehicles. Core temp above 104°F causes organ failure fast.

- Identify cooling centers before summer. Libraries, malls, shelters.
- Check on elderly neighbors daily during heat waves. Knock, don't call — phones get ignored.
- No AC: wet sheets, fan, cool showers, basement. Move to a cooling center.
- Drink water before thirst. Skip alcohol and caffeine.
- Heat stroke signs: hot skin, confusion, collapse. **Cool aggressively** — cold water immersion if possible, wet sheets, ice packs neck/armpits/groin. Call 911.
- Never leave kids, elderly, or pets in a parked car. Minutes.

## 3. Driving into floodwater

Over half of US flood deaths are people in vehicles. **6 inches** of moving water knocks an adult down. **12 inches** floats a small car. **18–24 inches** floats most trucks and SUVs.

- **Turn Around, Don't Drown.** No exceptions for "I know this road."
- Water over a road hides washed-out pavement underneath.
- Barricades exist because someone already died there.
- If trapped in a flooding car: out the window immediately, before water pressure seals the doors. Abandon the vehicle, move to high ground.
- Never drive around a barrier. Never walk into moving water above your knees.

## 4. Hypothermia in grid-down cold

People freeze in their own homes when central heat fails. Indoor temps drop into the 30s–40s within a day in a well-insulated house, faster in a poor one. Elderly and infants go first.

- **One-room strategy**: pick the smallest interior room, seal doors with blankets, live there.
- Dress in layers; wool and synthetics, not cotton. Hat indoors.
- Sleeping bag rated **below** your expected indoor low. Mylar blanket as a liner.
- Body-to-body warmth with family or pets.
- **Never** use an oven, stovetop burner, or unvented fuel heater for room heat. CO and fire.
- Insulate against the floor — cardboard, rug, foam pad. Cold floors pull heat fast.
- Shivering that stops without warming = severe hypothermia. Get help.

## 5. Smoke inhalation in fires

Kills roughly three times as many people as burns. Most residential fire deaths happen at night, victims overcome by smoke before flames reach the bedroom.

- **Working smoke alarms on every level and in every bedroom.** Test monthly, batteries yearly, replace unit every 10 years.
- **Close before you doze.** A closed bedroom door buys minutes and drops smoke/heat survivable levels.
- Two ways out of every room. Rehearse the escape plan with the whole household, including a meeting point outside.
- If smoke is present: stay low, crawl under it. Feel doors with the back of your hand before opening.
- Once out, stay out. No possessions, no pets, no re-entry.
- Kitchen fires: lid on the pan, heat off. Never water on grease.

## 6. Post-disaster cardiac events

Heart attack and stroke rates spike in the week after major disasters. Causes: exertion clearing debris, stress, dehydration, skipped medications, disrupted sleep. Snow-shoveling deaths are the textbook case.

- Fill prescriptions before the storm. Keep a **2-week buffer** of daily meds.
- Pace recovery work. Rest every 15 minutes. Hydrate. Sit if dizzy.
- Do not let a 65-year-old shovel a driveway or drag chainsawed limbs alone.
- Know the signs.
  - **Heart attack**: chest pressure, arm/jaw pain, shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea. Chew one adult-strength (325 mg) aspirin unless allergic or told not to by a doctor, then call 911.
  - **Stroke — FAST**: Face droop, Arm weakness, Speech slurred, Time to call 911. Note time of onset.
- Do not drive yourself. Wait for EMS.

## 7. Wound infection and sepsis

Cleanup produces cuts, punctures, and crush injuries. Floodwater contains sewage, fuel, and sharp debris. Delayed care turns a minor laceration into a limb or a life.

- **Tetanus shot current** — within 10 years, 5 years for dirty wounds. Get boosted before hurricane season if overdue.
- Irrigate wounds with **clean water under pressure** for several minutes. Soap around, not in. Cover with clean dressing.
- Watch for: spreading redness, red streaks up the limb, swelling, pus, fever, increasing pain. Any of these = seek care now.
- Puncture wounds, animal bites, and anything from floodwater need medical eval.
- If remote and a prescriber has given you standby antibiotics, follow their written plan.

## 8. Falls

Leading injury in the recovery phase. Roofs, ladders, trees, chainsaws, dark stairways, debris piles.

- Don't work on the roof in active weather. Ever.
- Ladders: **three points of contact**, base at 1:4 angle, tied off at top, on firm level ground, someone footing it.
- Chainsaw + tree work kills amateurs. If it's bigger than your leg or under tension, hire it out.
- Light every path after dark. Headlamp on, hands free.
- Stout boots, not sneakers, for cleanup.
- No roof, tree, or generator-on-a-slope work alone.

## 9. Suicide and overdose

Elevated for months to years after major disasters. Isolation, financial ruin, displacement, loss. Underappreciated and undercounted.

- **988** — Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text, US).
- Keep routines. Sleep, meals, light exercise, daylight.
- Check on people who live alone. Show up in person.
- If someone is struggling, **secure firearms and medications** off-site or in a locked container they don't have the key to. This single intervention saves lives.
- Limit alcohol during recovery. It worsens everything.
- Community, faith groups, neighbors — accept help and offer it.

## 10. Post-event drowning

Rip currents surge for days after hurricanes. Flash floods follow heavy rain hours after the sky clears. Rivers stay dangerous long after the crest.

- Stay out of the surf for **several days** after a tropical system — rip current deaths spike after the storm passes.
- Stay out of floodwater, period. Currents, debris, sewage, electrified wires, open manholes.
- Never let children play near swollen creeks, culverts, or storm drains.
- If caught in a rip: don't fight it, swim parallel to shore until free, then in.

## 11. Diarrheal disease from bad water

Globally the largest post-disaster killer. In the US less common but still present — boil-water notices exist for a reason, and compromised wells after floods are real.

- Assume any water of unknown integrity is contaminated.
- Treat: boil, bleach, filter + disinfect. See `water-purification.md`.
- Wash hands before food and after waste — soap and treated water, or alcohol sanitizer.
- Keep sick people hydrated — oral rehydration salts (ORS) or a homemade mix (1 L water, 6 tsp sugar, 1/2 tsp salt).
- Infants, elderly, and immunocompromised get medical care early.

## The pattern

Almost every death on this list is preventable with gear you already own, actions you already know, and decisions made before the event. Read the other cards. Rehearse with your household. Check on your neighbors.

The disaster is not what kills you. What kills you is the generator in the garage, the drive through the puddle, the shoveled driveway, the closed smoke alarm battery compartment, the skipped tetanus shot, the unchecked elderly neighbor.
