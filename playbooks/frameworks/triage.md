# Triage

Medical and resource triage when you cannot help everyone or fix everything.

## If you only read one line

**Fix the thing that kills fastest first.** Airway. Breathing. Bleeding. Then everything else.

## Medical triage

### The order, in minutes

| Order | Why | What to do |
|-------|-----|------------|
| 1. Airway | Obstructed airway kills in ~4 minutes | Position, clear, open. Head-tilt/chin-lift if no neck injury suspected. |
| 2. Breathing | Not breathing → brain damage in 4–6 min | Rescue breaths if trained. Chest rise is the test. |
| 3. Circulation / Bleeding | Arterial bleed kills in 3–5 min | Direct pressure; tourniquet for limbs if pressure fails. |
| 4. Shock | Minutes to hours | Lay flat, legs up (unless head/chest injury), warm, reassure. |
| 5. Fractures, burns | Hours | Immobilize, cover clean, don't reset major fractures without training. |
| 6. Soft tissue, minor cuts | Hours to days | Clean water flush, clean cover. Infection is the real risk. |

### START triage (multiple casualties)

When there are more patients than helpers, standard civilian emergency triage is START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment). Four categories:

- **Green (walking wounded)**: ambulatory, low priority. Ask them to move to a designated area.
- **Yellow (delayed)**: serious but stable. Can wait 1+ hour.
- **Red (immediate)**: life-threatening but treatable. Highest priority.
- **Black (expectant)**: not breathing after airway opening, or injuries incompatible with survival given resources. You do not treat them first. This is the hardest category psychologically.

**The test, in order:**

1. Can they walk? → Green.
2. Are they breathing? → If no, open airway once. Still no? → Black. If yes, → Red.
3. Respiratory rate >30/min? → Red.
4. No radial pulse, or capillary refill >2 seconds? → Red.
5. Cannot follow simple commands? → Red.
6. Otherwise → Yellow.

See `survival-guides/FM4-25.11_First_Aid_Manual.pdf` Chapter 1 for the US Army triage flow.

### Who gets the last dose of antibiotics

When resources are scarce, the person who will die without them and live with them gets them first. Not the sickest. Not the most beloved. The one for whom your intervention changes the outcome.

This is brutal. Make the call in advance:

- Adult septic with limited reserves: lower priority than a child with the same infection.
- Someone with a chronic terminal condition: not first in line.
- Yourself (the person doing the care): you must stay functional — second priority after the person whose life depends on you.

## Resource triage (water, food, heat, meds)

### Water

- **1st priority:** drinking water for everyone, 2L/person/day minimum.
- **2nd:** medical use (wound irrigation).
- **3rd:** food preparation.
- **4th:** hygiene (hand washing, face).
- **5th:** laundry, cleaning, flushing.

If running low: combine 3 and 4 (cook and then use the cool cooking water for hands). Skip 5.

### Food

- **Pregnant / breastfeeding women, infants, children, sick or injured** get fed first and most.
- **Working-age adults** next.
- **Healthy elderly** next.
- Calories matter more than variety in the short term. Fat is the highest-density calorie source.
- Salt is not optional in heat/sweat conditions. A person sweating for hours without salt replacement will cramp and collapse before they die of hunger.

### Heat

- **Infants and elderly** are most vulnerable to both cold and heat. They get the warm/cool room first.
- **Sick or injured** next — trauma and illness both impair temperature regulation.
- **Everyone else** adapts with clothing and behavior.

### Medication

- **Life-sustaining (insulin, seizure meds, heart meds, dialysis-related) before comfort meds.**
- **Maintenance doses before replacement doses.** Skipping a dose of blood-pressure medication is worse than skipping a painkiller.
- **Controlled substances must be secured** — desperation creates theft risk inside and outside your household.

See [`../tier-1-setup/01-household-roster.md`](../tier-1-setup/01-household-roster.md) for the medication log.

## Psychological triage

Not every stressed person needs the same response.

- **Frozen (dissociating, non-responsive):** simple tasks, short instructions, stay close.
- **Flooded (crying, panicked):** box breathing (4-in, 4-hold, 4-out, 4-hold), physical contact if welcome, remove from sensory overload.
- **Explosive (angry, aggressive):** space and distance, low voice, avoid commands, offer a choice.
- **Fine (alert, cooperative):** give them a job. They become your force multiplier.
- **Children:** routine and adult calm. Kids read adult fear instantly. A calm adult with a task is the single most effective pediatric crisis intervention.

See `medical/Psychological_First_Aid_Field_Operations_Guide.pdf` in the medical references.

## Group dynamics

- **One person decides. Discuss, then decide, then execute.** Committees under stress are worse than a slightly-wrong decisive leader.
- **Rotate the decider if the crisis extends past 24 hours.** Sleep deprivation kills judgment.
- **Name who does what.** "Alex, water. Sam, radio. Jordan, kids."
- **Check in on a clock, not when needed.** Every 3 hours in acute crisis, every 12 in extended shelter.

## Before it happens, do this once

- Take a Stop the Bleed course (free, ~90 min). See <https://www.stopthebleed.org/> while you have internet.
- Take a CPR course. Every household member 10+ should have one.
- Make the who-decides decision now, in writing, with a named backup.
- Run a 15-minute triage drill: someone rolls a scenario ("earthquake, 3 injured"), and you walk through priorities out loud. Awkward and useful.
