# Signalling and Rescue

How to be found when help is coming, and how to help others be found.

## If you only read one line

**Three of anything means distress.** Three whistles. Three fires. Three flashes. Three SOS dots (`... --- ...`). Rescuers are trained to recognize the pattern; random noises are ignored.

## When to signal

Signal when:

- You are lost or immobilized and help exists.
- You know rescue or aircraft are searching.
- You are in a defined search area (national park, known trail, post-disaster neighborhood being swept).

Do not signal when:

- You are in a hostile environment where attention is dangerous (active conflict, predatory groups).
- The signal cannot plausibly reach a responder (nobody is looking).
- You have not first confirmed your own position — signalling rescue to a spot *you* don't know won't save anyone.

## The hierarchy of signals

From strongest to weakest:

1. **Personal locator beacon (PLB) or EPIRB.** If you have one and it works, activate it. This is the single highest-leverage piece of outdoor gear per dollar.
2. **Cell phone (even with no bars).** 911 in the US, 112 internationally, will attempt emergency-only routing. SMS sometimes works where voice does not. Text a single trusted contact with coordinates + time.
3. **Ham or GMRS radio.** If you have one, use it. See [`../cards/radio-frequencies.md`](../cards/radio-frequencies.md) and `radio/ARRL_ARES_Field_Resources_Manual.pdf`.
4. **Whistle.** Three long blasts, pause, repeat. Audible for ~1 mile. Carries further than shouting and costs no energy.
5. **Signal mirror.** Reflects sunlight up to 10+ miles to aircraft. Aim by sighting through the center hole and catching the sun on your target.
6. **Smoke by day, fire by night.** Three fires in a triangle, 30 feet apart if possible. Green vegetation on a fire = white smoke. Rubber / oil = black smoke. Contrast against the terrain matters most.
7. **Ground-to-air symbols.** Large, high-contrast, on open ground. `V` = need assistance. `X` = need medical. `→` = going this direction. Use rocks, logs, clothing, stomped snow.
8. **Flashlight / headlamp.** At night, three flashes at regular intervals. Strobe mode visible from aircraft at long range.
9. **Voice.** Last resort. Tires out the caller and has short range.

## Location: the most important single thing

**Communicate your location before anything else.** A rescuer with no location cannot help you. Every signalling protocol assumes you know where you are.

- GPS coordinates (lat/long) if available.
- Grid reference on a paper map (see `survival-guides/FM3-25.26_Map_Reading_Land_Navigation.pdf`).
- Named road junction or landmark.
- Distance and direction from a known point (e.g., "~2 miles NE of the Bridal Veil Falls trailhead").

If truly lost: **stop moving.** You are harder to find if you wander. Go uphill if possible — height increases line-of-sight for signals. Near water is usually searched. Near a clear landmark is usually searched.

## STOP protocol (when lost)

1. **S**top. Sit down. Panic increases mistakes.
2. **T**hink. Last known position? Landmarks? Time since?
3. **O**bserve. What's around? Water? Shelter? Light?
4. **P**lan. Signal + stay, or move (only if confident in direction and daylight remaining).

Staying put is almost always correct. Search and rescue assumes a lost person stays near their last known location.

## Morse: the only code you need

If you can blink, blow, tap, or flash:

```
SOS = ... --- ...
       S     O     S
```

Three shorts, three longs, three shorts. Pause. Repeat. Works with any signal medium.

The response from a rescuer is often "K" (`-.-`), meaning "understood, keep sending." If you see or hear it, keep going.

## Making yourself visible from the air

- **Contrast:** use colors that stand out against your background. Orange and blue are visible against green/brown. Black against snow. Silver Mylar against anything.
- **Size:** a person is a dot from 1,000 feet. A 10-foot `V` of rocks is unmissable.
- **Motion:** waving a brightly colored item draws the eye. Static objects blend.
- **Shadow lines at low sun angle:** helicopter pilots often search at dawn or dusk. A trench or packed-snow arrow casts a long shadow.

## Search and rescue (SAR) patterns to know

- SAR typically searches outward from last known position in expanding concentric patterns.
- Helicopters fly at 500–1,000 ft AGL, line-of-sight only.
- Hasty teams hit trails, roads, and water courses first. Stay near one of those if you can.
- If you hear a vehicle, aircraft, or voices: signal *immediately* and *continuously*. The window is short. Three whistle blasts, mirror, smoke, whatever you have, repeatedly, until acknowledged.

## Marking a path when moving

If you are moving and hope to be tracked:

- Break branches at chest height on the side of your travel.
- Leave stacked-rock markers (cairns) at decision points.
- Scratch arrows in dirt or scrape bark.
- Leave a written note at your previous camp: where you went, when, and when to expect the next note.

## Getting help without electronics

If phones and radio are down and you must reach help physically:

- Move toward known infrastructure: road, rail line, river, power line.
- Follow water *downstream* in unfamiliar terrain — downstream usually leads to settlement.
- Travel in daylight. Night travel triples injury risk.
- Leave a note where you left: who, when, intended direction, expected return.

## Before it happens, do this once

- Buy a cheap whistle and attach it to your car keys, your kid's backpack, and your go-bag.
- Put a signal mirror in your go-bag (~$5).
- Learn SOS in Morse. You will not remember more than this code under stress; you don't need to.
- If you hike, carry a PLB or satellite messenger. Garmin inReach or similar is the gold standard for the cost.
- Program local repeaters, NOAA frequencies, and MURS/GMRS into your radio in advance.
