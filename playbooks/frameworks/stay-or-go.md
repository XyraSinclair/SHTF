# Stay or Go

The single most consequential decision in most emergencies. People die from guessing.

## If you only read one line

**Default to staying put** unless a specific, identifiable threat requires leaving. Movement in a crisis is expensive, dangerous, and usually unnecessary.

## Why "stay" is the default

Your home has water, food, tools, shelter, medication, documents, and people who know you. The road does not. In almost every modern disaster — Katrina, Maria, Sandy, Christchurch, Kobe, Fukushima — the people who stayed and sheltered did measurably better than the people who tried to leave during the event. Traffic jams, fuel shortages, accidents, and exposure kill more people than the disaster does.

Shelter-in-place is not passivity. It is the higher-probability survival strategy.

## When "go" is actually correct

Leave only if at least one of the following is true:

1. **Your shelter is structurally compromised.** Fire, flood, post-earthquake collapse risk, roof failure.
2. **A specific, directional hazard is arriving.** Wildfire bearing down, downstream flood, fallout plume with known direction and time, lahar, tsunami warning.
3. **The shelter itself will become lethal without intervention you cannot provide.** Extended heat wave with no power and no ventilation path; extended cold with no heat source and no insulation; smoke infiltration you cannot filter.
4. **Authorities with specific information have ordered evacuation** — not "recommend" but "order," and from a source you can verify (AM/FM broadcast, NOAA Weather Radio, official emergency broadcast).
5. **Continued presence exposes you to violence you cannot defend against.** Active combat zone, approaching armed group, not speculative civil unrest.

Notice what is **not** on this list: "things feel bad," "I saw something on social media," "a friend called worried."

## The decision tree

```
Is there an immediate directional threat (fire, flood, fallout plume, tsunami)?
├── YES ──► GO. Follow scenario playbook for that threat.
└── NO
    ├── Is your shelter structurally safe?
    │   ├── NO  ──► GO. To nearest safe structure, then reassess.
    │   └── YES
    │       ├── Do you have 72 hours of water, food, warmth, and meds?
    │       │   ├── NO  ──► Can you acquire them without traveling far?
    │       │   │         ├── YES ──► STAY. Acquire. Then reassess.
    │       │   │         └── NO  ──► Consider going to a known safer location.
    │       │   │                     Do NOT travel to an unknown one.
    │       │   └── YES ──► STAY. Shelter in place. Listen to radio.
    │       │               Reassess every 12 hours.
```

## If you go: where, how, and when

**Where:** a specific, identified destination with people expecting you. Not "somewhere away from the city." Pre-arranged family, friend, or rally point. See [`../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md`](../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md).

**How:** the shortest safe route, not the most scenic one. Main roads are crowded and exposed but better-maintained; back roads are faster in a normal crisis but impassable in a severe one. Decision: if the disaster is recent (hours), take main roads. If it's been days and infrastructure is degrading, take secondary routes.

**When:** as early as possible, or after the wave has passed. Do not leave during the peak — that's when the traffic kills you. "First day or fourth day, not second or third" is a rough rule derived from traffic studies of hurricane evacuations (see FEMA evacuation analyses).

**What to bring:** see [`../tier-1-setup/02-go-bag.md`](../tier-1-setup/02-go-bag.md). If unpacked: documents, cash, meds, water, warm layer, headlamp, knife, phone, paper map, charged battery.

**What to leave:** everything else. Sentimental items that do not fit in the car do not come. Pets come — they are family, not items.

## If you stay: what that actually means

Staying is not sitting still. It is a set of active protocols:

1. **Secure the shelter.** Close and latch windows. Identify a single designated room (interior, few windows, close to plumbing). Block gaps under doors if smoke/fallout/pandemic.
2. **Ration.** Assume the crisis lasts 3× longer than the broadcast estimate. Water first — 2L drinking per person per day minimum, plus 2L for sanitation (see `survival-guides/Emergency_Water_Purification_Guide.pdf`).
3. **Listen.** NOAA Weather Radio (162.400–162.550 MHz), or a battery/hand-crank AM radio. Stations: AM 1610 is the FEMA emergency frequency in some regions; check local. Do not rely on social media — it is misinformation-dense in crises.
4. **Check in.** Execute your family comms plan. Confirm everyone is accounted for, say you are staying, repeat location.
5. **Manage morale.** Kids and stressed adults need routine. Set a rough clock — meals at the same times, check-in every 3 hours, a task per person. Panic compounds.

## The trap: "we'll just get in the car and see"

This is the most common fatal decision pattern. Unplanned evacuations fail more than they succeed. If you have not pre-identified a destination, a route, and a trigger, you do not have an evacuation plan — you have a panic response.

Prevent this before anything happens: [`../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md`](../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md) forces you to write down a destination and a trigger now, while you are thinking clearly.

## Scenario defaults

| Scenario | Default |
|---------|---------|
| Tornado | STAY (interior, low, small room) |
| Hurricane | GO if mandatory order and ≥48h notice; else STAY |
| Earthquake | STAY (drop/cover/hold), then reassess structure |
| Wildfire approaching | GO as early as possible once direction is known |
| Flood rising | GO up. Vertical evacuation before horizontal. |
| Nuclear fallout | STAY, 48–72h minimum (NWSS Ch. 11) |
| Pandemic | STAY |
| Grid-down | STAY |
| Cyber collapse | STAY |
| Civil unrest nearby | STAY, lock down |
| House fire | GO immediately, no exceptions |
| Chemical plume | GO crosswind, not upwind or downwind |
| Tsunami warning | GO inland and uphill, immediately |
| Active shooter / violence | Run > Hide > Fight, in that order |

## Before it happens, do this once

1. Write down one primary and one backup evacuation destination. Confirm with the host.
2. Drive (not map — drive) the route at least once. Note fuel stations, hospitals, choke points.
3. Identify your designated shelter-in-place room inside your home.
4. Agree with household members on one phrase that means "execute the evacuation plan."
5. Pre-position go-bags near the door you would actually leave through.
