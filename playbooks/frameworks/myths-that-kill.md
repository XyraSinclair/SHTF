# Myths That Kill

Common survival advice that is wrong and dangerous. Each entry: the myth, why it's wrong, what to do instead.

## If you only read one line

**Confident bad advice is more common than honest uncertainty.** When a source sounds certain and simple, pressure-test it against a second source before betting your life on it.

## Water and food

### Myth: "You can drink water from a stream if it looks clean."

**Wrong.** Giardia, cryptosporidium, and bacteria have no color or smell. Diarrhea in an emergency is not an inconvenience — it is a primary killer through dehydration.

**Do:** filter (to 0.2 microns) AND disinfect (boil 1 minute at sea level, 3 minutes above 6,500 ft; or 2 drops unscented household bleach per liter, wait 30 min). See `survival-guides/Emergency_Water_Purification_Guide.pdf`.

### Myth: "Eating snow keeps you hydrated."

**Wrong.** Eating snow in cold conditions accelerates hypothermia (your body spends calories melting it) and can actually dehydrate you on net. It also rarely provides enough volume.

**Do:** melt snow first using body heat in a bottle inside your jacket, or over a fire. Then drink.

### Myth: "The rule of threes: three days without water."

**Half-true, dangerous in practice.** The "three days" figure assumes rest, temperate climate, and no exertion. In heat or activity, severe dehydration can happen in 12 hours. Kidney damage starts earlier than death.

**Do:** 2 liters per person per day is the sustainment minimum; 4L+ in heat or activity. Don't ration thirst — ration sweat (slow down, shade, cover skin).

### Myth: "You can eat anything the animals eat."

**Wrong.** Squirrels eat amanita mushrooms. Birds eat poison ivy berries. Animal metabolism does not map to human metabolism.

**Do:** only eat plants you can positively identify (see `food-water/Washington_State_Foraging_Guide.pdf` for PNW). When in doubt, go hungry — you can last weeks without food.

## Medical

### Myth: "Suck the venom out of a snakebite."

**Wrong.** Suction removes negligible venom and introduces infection via the mouth. Cutting the wound makes it worse.

**Do:** keep the bitten limb below heart level, remove jewelry, mark the swelling edge with a pen and note the time, get to medical care. Tourniquet only for known neurotoxic species and only as last resort (see WTND p. 104–106).

### Myth: "Put butter / ice / toothpaste on a burn."

**Wrong.** Butter holds heat against the tissue. Ice causes frostbite damage on top of the burn. Toothpaste has no medical basis.

**Do:** cool running water (not ice) for 10–20 minutes, cover with clean non-stick dressing, do not pop blisters. See FM4-25.11 burn section.

### Myth: "Tilt your head back for a nosebleed."

**Wrong.** Sends blood into the throat and stomach, causing nausea and masking blood loss.

**Do:** lean forward, pinch the soft part of the nose for 10 full minutes without checking.

### Myth: "Rub a frostbitten hand with snow to warm it."

**Wrong.** Damages the already-compromised tissue.

**Do:** immerse in 100–108°F (38–42°C) water until soft and colored. Do not thaw if there is any chance of refreezing — refreezing causes far worse damage than staying frozen longer.

### Myth: "Potassium iodide protects you from radiation."

**Dangerously partial truth.** Potassium iodide (KI) saturates the thyroid gland against radioactive iodine-131 only. It does nothing against cesium-137, strontium-90, plutonium, gamma exposure from fallout particles, or blast effects.

**Do:** KI is useful specifically near a nuclear reactor accident releasing I-131 (Fukushima, Chernobyl) and specifically for thyroid protection in people under 40 and children. For fallout from a weapon, the protection is *shelter mass*, not pills. See NWSS Ch. 11 and CDC KI guidance.

### Myth: "A tourniquet costs you the limb."

**Wrong, and this myth causes deaths.** Modern tourniquets (CAT, SOF-T) applied for up to 2 hours have very high limb-salvage rates. Uncontrolled arterial bleeding kills in minutes.

**Do:** if bleeding is arterial (spurting, pooling fast) and direct pressure does not control it, apply a tourniquet 2–3 inches proximal to the wound, tighten until bleeding stops, mark the time. See [`../cards/stop-the-bleed.md`](../cards/stop-the-bleed.md).

### Myth: "Give CPR to anyone unconscious."

**Wrong.** CPR on a beating heart is harmful and can stop it. Confirm no pulse / no breathing first.

**Do:** check responsiveness, check breathing for up to 10 seconds. If neither, start compressions (100–120/min, center of chest, 2 inches deep). See FM4-25.11 and local first-aid course.

## Fire and heat

### Myth: "Open a door to check if a fire is spreading."

**Wrong.** Opening a door provides oxygen and can flash-over a smoldering fire, trapping you. Back-draft is real.

**Do:** back of the hand to the door. Hot = do not open. See `scenarios/01-house-fire.md`.

### Myth: "Jump out a second-story window."

**Usually wrong.** Broken legs in a fire are a near-death sentence. Hanging from a windowsill before dropping cuts fall distance by half.

**Do:** if you must exit from a second story, hang first, aim for soft ground, bend knees and roll. First-story windows are always preferable.

## Radiation and fallout

### Myth: "Lead is the only thing that stops radiation."

**Wrong.** Any dense mass stops gamma radiation. Earth is cheap and abundant. Two feet of packed dirt provides roughly the same gamma attenuation as 4 inches of lead.

**Do:** NWSS Ch. 5 covers expedient earth shelters. Mass matters more than material.

### Myth: "Fallout kills everything for decades."

**Wrong, and this myth causes people to give up prematurely.** Fallout radioactivity decays on the "7/10 rule": radiation drops to 1/10 of its initial rate at 7 hours, 1/100 at 49 hours, 1/1000 at 2 weeks. The first 48 hours are by far the most dangerous.

**Do:** shelter for 48–72 hours minimum. After that, exposure is manageable if you stay out of heavy fallout zones. See NWSS Ch. 1 and Ch. 11.

### Myth: "If you see the flash, you are already dead."

**Wrong.** The flash is visible from hundreds of miles. Fatal effects from a single weapon are typically within a radius of a few miles. If you see a flash: drop, cover, do not look, wait for the blast wave, then assess.

**Do:** see [`../scenarios/08-nuclear.md`](../scenarios/08-nuclear.md).

## Social and psychological

### Myth: "Other people are the danger."

**Mostly wrong, in ways that cause bad decisions.** In actual disasters, cooperation rises and crime falls. The Katrina myths about mass looting and violence were largely media-driven and later disproven. You are more likely to survive with neighbors than against them.

**Do:** identify your neighbors *before* the emergency. A shared block is a far better asset than a hidden bunker.

### Myth: "Stay calm" is enough.

**Insufficient.** Saying "stay calm" to a stressed person does nothing. Structure does.

**Do:** give people tasks. Set a clock. Break problems into 20-minute chunks. Children need a job and a routine more than they need a speech.

## Gear

### Myth: "More gear is better."

**Wrong.** Gear you have not used under stress is unlikely to work for you under stress. Skill beats kit.

**Do:** own less, practice more. Start a fire with a ferro rod in your backyard. Apply a tourniquet to a willing family member. Run a water filter on creek water. If the first time you use something is in an emergency, you will use it badly.

### Myth: "I need a bug-out location in the wilderness."

**Usually wrong for most people.** Living off the land requires skills, calories, and tools most people do not have. A pre-arranged friend or family member at 100 miles distance is a more realistic plan than an unmaintained cabin.

**Do:** see stay-or-go framework. Most people's best shelter is their home.
