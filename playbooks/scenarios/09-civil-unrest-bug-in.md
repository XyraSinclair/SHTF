# Civil Unrest (Bug-In)

Protest activity, rioting, or localized violence near your home or neighborhood. The correct response is almost always to stay home and not be part of the story.

## If you only read one line

**Don't be there.** The single most effective intervention is to not be in the area. Re-route your life around it for as long as it lasts. Most civil unrest resolves in days.

## What this is and isn't

**This playbook covers:**

- Protests that may turn confrontational.
- Riots, looting, or property destruction near you.
- Post-incident tensions (police shooting, political event, widely-publicized trigger event).
- Temporary breakdown of routine law enforcement presence.

**This playbook does not cover:**

- Active shooter events (see local law enforcement guidance: Run → Hide → Fight).
- Home invasion (distinct threat, different response — locally legal self-defense measures).
- War or armed conflict at a scale that makes "civil unrest" an understatement — that's a different playbook, not this one, and you'd be reading a different document.

## Detection

- Local news and police scanners (available via apps like Broadcastify) give earliest warning.
- Social media during civil unrest is heavily amplified — filter to local, verified sources.
- Your neighborhood social apps (Nextdoor, Ring neighbors) are useful for immediate proximity but unreliable beyond.
- Police radio traffic surges hours before public media picks up.

## Decision: stay or go

See [`../frameworks/stay-or-go.md`](../frameworks/stay-or-go.md). For civil unrest specifically:

**Stay is almost always correct.** Reasons to go anyway:

- Active structure fires in your building or adjacent.
- Confirmed armed threat specifically targeting your location (rare).
- Official evacuation order (rare for civil unrest; more common for adjacent scenarios like large fires triggered by unrest).

Reasons to definitely stay:

- You are far from the epicenter and have supplies.
- Travel would put you closer to the unrest than staying.
- You have vulnerable household members (kids, elderly, disabled) who would be harder to move than to shelter.

## First hour

1. **Account for everyone in your household.** Where are they? Do they know not to come toward the unrest?
2. **Close and lock all doors, windows, and garage.**
3. **Close blinds and curtains** on street-facing windows. Don't advertise presence.
4. **Move vehicles into garage** if possible, or off the street.
5. **Text out-of-area contact** (see [`../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md`](../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md)): location, status, intent.
6. **Tune radio or news** to local broadcast for verified updates.
7. **Do not go outside for a better view. Do not livestream. Do not shout commentary.** Anonymity is your friend.

## First 24 hours

### Staying unobtrusive

- Minimal visible activity.
- Lights on normal patterns — not all dark (looks vacant), not unusually bright (stands out).
- If fire or smoke nearby, prepare to evacuate if it approaches. See [`01-house-fire.md`](01-house-fire.md) and [`04-wildfire-evacuation.md`](04-wildfire-evacuation.md).
- Lock interior doors to rooms you're not in. Slows any intruder; buys time to call 911.
- If violence reaches your block: shelter in an interior room, below window level, call 911.

### Who you let in

- Don't open the door for strangers.
- Verify identity even for neighbors (people under stress mis-identify).
- Uniformed police, fire, or medics: open door for them, but with a witness and phone camera on.
- People seeking shelter: your judgment. Most civil unrest participants are not dangerous; some are. Default to not opening unless you recognize them.

### Kids

- Keep them in an interior room if possible.
- Explain calmly what's happening without panic imagery.
- Normal activities: eating, reading, screen time, sleep routine.
- Do not show them live coverage of the unrest.

### Pets

- Keep inside. Dogs may bark at noise; minimize stimulation.
- Have leashes and carriers ready in case evacuation becomes necessary.

## Longer duration (days)

If unrest persists more than 24 hours in your area:

### Supplies

- Delay non-essential shopping. Order delivery if available and roads are safe.
- Keep tank above half; avoid refueling if stations are near unrest.
- Your 14-day stockpile (see [`../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md`](../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md)) is enough.

### Routine

- Maintain a loose routine for household mental health.
- Brief updates from reliable sources a few times per day, not continuous consumption.
- Physical activity indoors: small workouts, stretching, stairs.
- Work from home if you can; some employers will excuse attendance for documented local unrest.

### Communication

- Text-based communication preserves attention; phone calls consume it.
- Your out-of-area contact can relay to extended family — you don't need to update everyone directly.

## Specific threats

### Looting

- Rarely targets occupied homes in residential neighborhoods. Commercial streets, retail corridors, and unattended properties are the pattern.
- If you live above a business: your occupied apartment is usually safer than the store below. Do not try to defend the store.
- If looting reaches your residential block: interior room, phone 911, do not confront.

### Fire nearby

- Fire is the most serious secondary risk in civil unrest.
- Be ready to evacuate to your pre-planned destination.
- Embers from adjacent structures can ignite yours; see [`04-wildfire-evacuation.md`](04-wildfire-evacuation.md) for structure-hardening concepts that also apply to urban fire.

### Tear gas or crowd-control agents drifting into your area

- Close windows, vents.
- Wet towels at door gaps.
- Shower if exposed (rinse clothing, rinse eyes with water).
- Fresh air outside clears it in 20–30 minutes once deployment stops.
- Milk-in-eyes is a myth; water is the correct rinse.

### Power or utility disruption

- Infrastructure is occasionally damaged during unrest.
- Treat as grid-down (see [`05-grid-down-extended.md`](05-grid-down-extended.md)).

### Armed individuals in your neighborhood

- Call 911. State specifically: "I see armed person(s) at [location]. I am at [address]. I will stay inside."
- Do not open the door to armed non-uniformed people even if they claim to be authorities.
- Stay away from windows, below sight lines.

## Self-defense

Laws on self-defense vary dramatically by jurisdiction. This document cannot substitute for understanding your local laws.

General principles:

- Escape is always legally preferable to engagement.
- Most home-defense scenarios involve communicating with 911 while sheltering in an interior room. Confrontation is last resort.
- If you own firearms: know your state's "castle doctrine," duty-to-retreat rules, and use-of-force thresholds. Train, store safely, and understand the legal exposure.
- Less-lethal options (pepper spray, tasers) are legal in more jurisdictions than firearms, and are effective for most realistic threats short of armed confrontation.

This is not a self-defense tutorial. If you are serious about it, take a course locally and consult a lawyer.

## Social media during unrest

- Don't post photos/videos that identify your exact location.
- Don't announce that you're home alone.
- Don't share unverified claims ("there's a mob at X") — you may be amplifying false information.
- Don't engage with combative strangers online during the event; it invites doxxing.
- If you have sensitive political associations on the block with your address (bumper stickers, signs, social posts), consider removing them before or during unrest.

## Photographing or recording

- Your phone is useful for documenting damage after the fact.
- Do not photograph crowd members with the intent to identify them — this is legally risky and socially risky.
- If you are detained or questioned by police: remain calm, do not resist, state "I'd like to speak with a lawyer" and stop answering questions beyond identification.

## Legal aftermath

- If your property was damaged: photograph everything, file police report, file insurance claim.
- Insurance policies vary on riot/civil-unrest coverage — check your declaration page.
- Federal disaster declarations sometimes extend to civil unrest; check FEMA for your area.

## After it subsides

- Reassess your risk profile. Is this recurring? Will future events escalate?
- Some choose to relocate after repeated civil unrest near them. That's a real decision; weigh honestly.
- Community resilience: know your neighbors better than you do now. Shared watchfulness is more effective than individual fortification.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. Identify your "safe interior room" — in civil unrest contexts, an interior room with no street-facing windows, near a bathroom and water.
2. Know your local laws on self-defense, reporting threats, and firearm storage.
3. Subscribe to local emergency alerts (county, city).
4. Have a plan with your household: if unrest breaks out while members are apart, who goes where.
5. Know two ways out of your neighborhood by car, and one on foot.
6. Meet your neighbors. Shared awareness is your biggest asset.
