# Nuclear

Four distinct sub-scenarios with different protocols. People consistently conflate them and act wrongly.

This is a digest of `survival-guides/Nuclear_War_Survival_Skills.pdf` (NWSS, Cresson Kearny, Oak Ridge National Laboratory — the authoritative civilian source). Read the chapters cited below when you have time before something happens.

## If you only read one line

**Shelter mass and time are what save you.** For fallout, two feet of packed earth (or equivalent mass) overhead, for 48 hours minimum. For blast, distance and intervening structure. Pills and rumors do not save you; mass and time do.

## The four sub-scenarios

| Scenario | Primary threat | Protocol |
|---------|----------------|----------|
| **Distant fallout** | Radioactive dust arriving hours later | Shelter-in-place, 48–72h, seal building |
| **Near-miss airburst** | Blast, thermal flash, then possibly fallout | Drop/cover immediately, then shelter |
| **Dirty bomb (RDD)** | Localized radiological contamination, mostly panic | Move upwind, minimize inhalation, decon |
| **Nuclear power plant accident** | Iodine-131, other isotopes | Shelter, potassium iodide if close, follow authority instructions |

## Sub-scenario 1: Distant fallout

You are **tens to hundreds of miles** from a detonation. Shock wave may have passed hours ago or not reached you. Radioactive fallout is traveling with the wind and will arrive in hours.

### Detection

- Emergency broadcast confirms detonation.
- News or NOAA Weather Radio plots fallout plume.
- Geiger counter, if you have one, registers rising background radiation.

### First hour

1. **Get indoors.** Any structure is better than none; basement or interior ground floor is best.
2. **Seal the shelter.** Close windows, doors, vents. Tape heavy plastic over gaps if you have time. Weather-strip matters.
3. **Identify your shielded spot.** Maximum mass between you and outdoors and above you. Basement corner, inner bathroom, under a heavy desk if there's no basement.
4. **Water**: fill everything before pressure drops. Tub, sinks, buckets, bottles.
5. **Food and supplies**: bring everything reachable indoors.
6. **Do not go outdoors after fallout begins** without protective precautions. Fallout looks like gray dust or sand.

### The 7/10 rule (critical)

Fallout radiation decays quickly at first:

- 7 hours after detonation: radiation is **1/10** of what it was at 1 hour.
- 49 hours (7 × 7): radiation is **1/100** of what it was at 1 hour.
- 2 weeks: approximately **1/1000**.

Translation: the first 24–48 hours are by far the most dangerous. Staying sheltered through them is what saves you.

Citation: NWSS Chapter 1.

### The next 48–72 hours

- **Stay sheltered.** Do not leave unless structure compromised.
- Minimize door openings.
- Ration water (4 L/person/day baseline — drinking, cooking, minimal hygiene).
- Sanitation: bucket toilet with lid, sealed in a remote part of shelter. Dust off shoes before re-entry if someone must go out.
- Listen to radio. Emergency broadcasts will include "OK to leave shelter" and designated roads.
- Monitor for symptoms: nausea, vomiting, weakness. Short-term these can indicate radiation sickness. Severe cases require medical care, if available.

### After 48–72 hours

- External radiation has dropped enough for short outdoor exposures (tens of minutes) in most areas.
- Start gradual ventilation of shelter.
- Do not eat food that was uncovered during fallout.
- Water from sealed containers is fine. Tap water may be contaminated via open reservoirs.
- Authorities will issue updated guidance on exposure limits for outdoor activity.

### Expedient shelter (if you have no basement)

NWSS Chapter 5 details **expedient earth shelters** built in a few hours if warning time permits:

- Trench shelter: 4 ft deep, covered with poles and 2 ft of earth.
- Door-covered trench: interior door on poles, covered with plastic and earth.
- Above-ground box shelter with earth walls.

Two feet of packed earth blocks approximately 99% of initial gamma radiation. Earth is cheap and everywhere.

## Sub-scenario 2: Near-miss airburst

You see a flash so bright it lights the sky even in daylight. If you are within visible range, you are *probably* far enough to survive the blast.

### The next seconds

1. **Drop to the ground.** Face down. Hands over ears. Close your eyes.
2. **Do not look at the flash.** It can flash-blind you.
3. **Get behind any mass** — a car, a wall, a ditch. Anything reduces thermal and overpressure exposure.
4. **Stay down.** Shock wave may arrive seconds to a minute after the flash depending on distance.
5. **After the wave passes:** check yourself for injuries. Check others. Move away from collapsing structures.

### First 15 minutes

- **Move to shelter** — solid building, away from broken glass and downed wires.
- **Decon yourself and clothing**: brush off outer layer, ideally remove and bag outer clothing, wash exposed skin with soap and water if available. Do not use hot water (opens pores). Do not use harsh chemicals.
- **Avoid looking at the fireball** — retinal damage can occur from the thermal pulse even at significant distance.

### Then, switch to the fallout protocol above

Fallout from an airburst at ground level reaches downwind areas within 15 min to several hours. Shelter in place and follow the 7/10 rule.

### If caught in the open during blast wave

- Overpressure injures via flying glass and debris more than direct blast in most cases.
- Face down, hands over ears (prevents eardrum rupture), eyes closed.
- Low, flat, behind any object.

Citation: NWSS Chapter 11 (blast and thermal), Chapter 1 (overview).

## Sub-scenario 3: Dirty bomb (radiological dispersal device)

A conventional explosive wraps radioactive material. Localized contamination, mostly within city blocks. Primary danger is inhalation of radioactive dust during the event and for hours after.

### First 10 minutes

1. **Move upwind from the blast site.** Away, not through.
2. **Cover mouth and nose** — shirt, bandana, anything. Breathe shallowly.
3. **Leave the immediate area (blocks, not miles).**
4. **Get indoors** once you're a few blocks upwind.

### First hour

- **Remove outer clothing** and bag. This eliminates 80–90% of contamination on your person.
- **Shower** with soap and water. No hot water. Wash hair; do not use conditioner (binds contamination to hair).
- **Stay indoors** until authorities issue guidance.
- Follow official broadcasts for evacuation routes or shelter recommendations.

### What not to do

- Don't immediately evacuate by car without guidance. Panic evacuations cause more deaths than dirty bombs typically do (the radiological impact is usually limited).
- Don't take potassium iodide. KI protects only against radioactive iodine-131, which dirty bombs rarely contain.
- Don't assume the worst. Most credible dirty-bomb scenarios result in cleanup, not mass casualty.

## Sub-scenario 4: Nuclear power plant accident

Reactor breach or meltdown releases radioactive isotopes — particularly iodine-131, cesium-137.

### Detection

- Official emergency notification from plant or state.
- Evacuation zones typically defined at 10-mile (plume exposure) and 50-mile (food ingestion) radii from the plant.

### First hour

- If within 10 miles and instructed to **shelter**: seal building, stay inside, turn off HVAC.
- If within 10 miles and instructed to **evacuate**: follow designated routes.
- If outside 10 miles: monitor, usually no action required.

### Potassium iodide (KI)

- **KI is useful specifically for nuclear power plant accidents** releasing I-131.
- KI saturates the thyroid, preventing uptake of radioactive iodine.
- Dose (per FDA, verify current guidance): adults 130 mg, children 65 mg, infants 32 mg, per day while exposure ongoing.
- Most effective when taken *before* or within hours of exposure.
- **Limitations**: KI does nothing against other isotopes, external radiation, blast. Only thyroid protection. Only iodine.
- **Contraindications**: iodine allergy, dermatitis herpetiformis, hypocomplementemic vasculitis. Talk to a doctor about pre-existing thyroid conditions.
- Distributed free by some states near reactors; available OTC in most places.

**For nuclear weapons (not reactors)**, KI is less clearly useful — fallout isotope composition is different and initial shelter is the dominant intervention.

### Food and water

- Food grown near the accident may accumulate radioactive isotopes, especially dairy (I-131 concentrates in milk).
- FDA and state authorities issue guidance on food bans.
- Stored food purchased before the event is fine.

### Longer term

- Contaminated zones may be exclusion areas for decades (Chernobyl, Fukushima example).
- If your area is designated evacuation, evacuate. Do not attempt to "ride it out" near an active release.

## Radiation measurement

- **Geiger counter**: detects alpha, beta, gamma. Useful but not required — cheap units are ~$150, quality ones $400+.
- **Dosimeter**: personal exposure badge, tracks cumulative dose. Under $50 for simple ones.
- **Dose limits** (per NWSS Ch. 12 and NCRP):
  - Occupational limit: 5 rem/year.
  - Emergency worker limit: 25 rem (lifesaving missions).
  - Whole-body dose for severe illness: 100+ rem.
  - Whole-body lethal dose: 400+ rem (LD50).
- Most civilians will never accurately measure their exposure. Shelter and time discipline matters far more than measurement.

## Radiation sickness triage

Acute exposure symptoms roughly by dose:

- **<50 rem**: no acute symptoms.
- **50–200 rem**: nausea, vomiting hours after. Fatigue. Recovery likely without major medical intervention.
- **200–400 rem**: marked GI symptoms, immune suppression. Medical care needed; mortality 5–50% depending on care.
- **400+ rem**: severe GI and hematologic effects. High mortality even with best care.
- **1000+ rem**: near-certain fatality regardless of care.

In mass-casualty scenarios, triage follows established protocols: the severely irradiated who will die regardless receive comfort care; the moderately exposed receive intensive support; the lightly exposed get monitoring.

See [`../frameworks/triage.md`](../frameworks/triage.md) and NWSS Chapter 13.

## Communications during and after

- AM/FM radio is the primary broadcast authority. EAS and NOAA Weather Radio carry official messages.
- Cell networks likely overwhelmed or damaged by EMP (see below).
- Ham / GMRS radio continues to function; other operators may relay information.

## EMP (electromagnetic pulse)

- A high-altitude nuclear detonation can produce EMP damaging unshielded electronics over large areas. This is real but less uniformly severe than some prepper literature suggests.
- Most vehicles, radios, and appliances continue functioning. Long transmission lines can be damaged, affecting grid.
- Protection: any complete metal enclosure (faraday cage) — metal trash can lined with cardboard, sealed ammo can — shields contents. Stored spare radio, flashlight, battery, small laptop in a Faraday setup is modest insurance.

Citation: NWSS discusses EMP in Appendix B.

## What you do not need

- A bunker in the mountains. Shelter at home or a nearby sturdy structure is almost always better than fleeing to unmaintained remote locations.
- Gas masks (useful briefly against particulate fallout, but sealed shelter is the dominant intervention). A basic N95 or P100 respirator covers particulate inhalation adequately for most citizen scenarios.
- Specialty food rations. Your 14-day food store is enough for the dominant-exposure period.

## Prevention is mostly impossible

Unlike other scenarios, nuclear incidents are largely outside individual prevention. What you can do:

- Know your proximity to likely target areas (major military bases, political centers) and nuclear facilities.
- Know prevailing winds (fallout downwind direction).
- Be informed politically if you care to engage.
- Do not participate in panic. Measured response saves more lives than flight.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. Read NWSS. The PDF is in `survival-guides/Nuclear_War_Survival_Skills.pdf`. Chapters 1, 5, and 11 are the core.
2. Identify where in your home has the most shielding mass (basement, interior rooms, under stairs).
3. Stock your 14-day kit per [`../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md`](../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md). This works for fallout shelter too.
4. If within 20 miles of a nuclear power plant: get potassium iodide, know your evacuation route and sirens.
5. Keep a hand-crank AM/FM/NOAA radio.
6. Tape and heavy plastic in your supplies: cheap, useful for sealing shelter against fallout.
7. Have a plan conversation with your household. Rehearse shelter-in-place once; the "drop, cover, move to shelter" sequence should not be the first time you do it under stress.
