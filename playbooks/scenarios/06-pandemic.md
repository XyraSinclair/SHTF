# Pandemic

The scenario we have the most recent data on (COVID-19), and the one where your response matters most in the first 72 hours of public awareness.

## If you only read one line

**Disease spreads through air, surfaces, and close contact before it spreads through news headlines. By the time a pandemic is declared, it has been transmitting for weeks. Act on early signals, not confirmed cases.**

## Detection — signals worth acting on

- WHO declaring a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC).
- Case clusters of unidentified respiratory / GI illness in multiple countries or regions.
- A country locking down a major city or region.
- Airlines canceling routes into a specific area.
- Healthcare workers reporting PPE shortages or unusual patient loads.
- Clade / variant news about increased transmissibility, immune evasion, or severity.

When multiple signals appear concurrently, start Tier 1 actions below even if official posture is "low concern."

## Timeline of a pandemic you are living through

### Weeks before it's named

- Unusual illness reports in a region.
- Quiet concern in infectious disease communities (follow accounts on public platforms: CIDRAP, major epidemiologists, WHO situation reports).

### Weeks 0–4 (emerging)

- First confirmed local cases.
- Public response uneven; supply chains normal.
- **This is your prep window.** Top up stockpile, PPE, meds.

### Weeks 4–12 (active spread)

- Public awareness spikes, panic buying begins.
- PPE, certain foods, and medications stock out.
- Elective medical care delayed.
- Schools and workplaces start closing.

### Weeks 12+ (sustained / waves)

- New baseline forms. Masks, testing, some restrictions.
- Healthcare capacity strained.
- Economic and social disruption settles into a rhythm.
- Waves: second and subsequent waves often involve variants. Immunity from prior infection or vaccination wanes and/or is partial.

## First 72 hours of local pandemic awareness

1. **Top up stockpile** before panic buying hits. See [`../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md`](../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md). Add: extra shelf-stable food, comfort items, cleaning supplies.
2. **Medications**: 90-day supplies if your prescription and insurance allow. Ask doctor about extending.
3. **PPE**: N95 or KN95 respirators (5–10 per person, more if you will be out regularly), nitrile gloves (1 box per household), hand sanitizer (2–3 bottles), surface disinfectant.
4. **OTC meds**: acetaminophen, ibuprofen, antipyretics for kids, cough suppressants, oral rehydration salts, thermometer (digital, two of them).
5. **Test kits**: if rapid at-home tests exist for the pathogen, stock 10+ per person.
6. **Identify who in your household or neighborhood is highest risk** — elderly, immunocompromised, pregnant. Their protection drives your behavior.
7. **Establish a "clean home" protocol** (see below).

## Transmission basics

Most respiratory pandemics spread through some combination of:

- **Airborne** (fine aerosols): travels distances, accumulates in poorly ventilated spaces. Mitigated by ventilation, filtration, respirators.
- **Droplet** (short-range large particles): within ~6 feet of a symptomatic person. Mitigated by distance and surgical-style masks.
- **Contact** (touching contaminated surfaces then face): less important than initially thought for COVID-19; more important for norovirus and some enteric pathogens.
- **Fecal-oral**: key for enteric pandemics (cholera, some flu-like variants). Hand washing, food safety, water treatment.

**Interventions that actually work**, in rough order of effect:

1. Staying out of high-density poorly-ventilated indoor spaces with strangers.
2. Ventilation (outdoor air exchange, open windows, filtration).
3. Properly fit N95 / KN95 respirators.
4. Hand hygiene.
5. Vaccination (if available, for pathogens with effective vaccines).
6. Distancing.
7. Surface disinfection (less important than initially taught for most respiratory pathogens).

## "Clean home" protocol for a respiratory pandemic

Designate a single entry point. At that entry point:

- Shoes off.
- Mask off, into a designated bag or bin.
- Hand wash immediately, for 20+ seconds with soap.
- Outerwear off, hung in a designated zone.
- Change clothes if you were in high-risk environment.

Groceries and packages:

- For most respiratory pathogens, surface decon is low-priority. Don't wipe every can. Wash hands after handling.
- Perishables handled normally; cook food appropriately.

Ventilation:

- Open windows periodically even in winter. 5–10 minutes of airflow once or twice a day changes room air substantially.
- Run HVAC with MERV-13+ filter.
- DIY air cleaners (Corsi-Rosenthal box: 20" box fan + four MERV-13 filters + cardboard frame, ~$80) are one of the highest-leverage cheap interventions.

Testing and isolation:

- If symptomatic or exposed: test if tests available; isolate from household best you can.
- Designate one bathroom for sick person if possible.
- Sick person wears mask when out of designated room; household members mask when entering that area.
- Clean shared surfaces (doorknobs, faucets, toilet handles) daily during active illness.

## When to seek medical care

For most respiratory pandemics, the triage is:

- **Go to ER immediately**: severe shortness of breath, lips or nail beds turning blue, confusion or loss of consciousness, severe chest pain, uncontrolled bleeding, seizures, dehydration with inability to keep fluids down.
- **Call doctor or telehealth**: fever >101°F for >3 days, shortness of breath on mild exertion, symptoms worsening after initial improvement (possible secondary infection), known high-risk factors (immunosuppression, pregnancy, elderly, chronic cardiac / pulmonary disease).
- **Manage at home**: mild fever, cough, sore throat, fatigue without red-flag symptoms. Rest, fluids, antipyretics, monitor oxygen with pulse oximeter if available.

**Pulse oximeter** (<$30) is a high-value tool in a respiratory pandemic. Healthy resting SpO2 is 95%+. Below 92% at rest is concerning; below 88% is urgent. Home monitoring caught many silent hypoxia cases in COVID-19.

## Household isolation

If one member is sick:

- Separate bedroom if possible; separate bathroom if possible.
- Sick person uses separate dishes and utensils, or wash immediately after use.
- Laundry handled with gloves; wash on normal cycle (heat kills most respiratory pathogens on fabric).
- Sick person masks when leaving isolation room.
- Household members mask when entering isolation area.
- Monitor symptoms and oxygen.
- Notify anyone outside the household with whom sick member had recent close contact.

## Supply chain and shortages

Early pandemics disrupt supply chains in predictable ways:

- **First week**: panic buying of paper products, cleaning supplies, rice, beans, certain OTC meds.
- **First month**: shortages of PPE, specific foods tied to affected regions, baby formula (historical pattern).
- **Sustained**: some prescription medications (especially those manufactured in one region), certain medical supplies, targeted imports.

Protecting against this: Tier 1 stockpile is usually sufficient for the first-month shortages. For prescription meds, talk to doctor about 90-day supplies or alternative sources.

## Work and school

- **Remote work**: if your job allows it, remote is safer. Early in a pandemic, remote-ready is a measurable survival advantage.
- **Kids in school**: individual risk is lower, household exposure is much higher. Parents of school-age kids live with higher transmission risk.
- **Essential workers**: PPE discipline, commute risk management, decontamination on arrival home become critical.
- **Vulnerable household members**: consider whether anyone in the household needs to shield more strictly (e.g., immunocompromised grandparent means stricter in-home mitigations).

## Mental health

Sustained pandemic is mentally corrosive. Documented patterns from COVID-19:

- Isolation worsens depression and anxiety in most people.
- Alcohol and drug misuse spike.
- Domestic violence rises.
- Children regress socially and academically.
- Healthcare worker burnout is severe and long-lasting.

What helps:

- **Routine**: fixed meal times, sleep schedule, daily outdoor time.
- **Outdoor exposure**: outdoor socializing with safe people is much lower risk than indoor, and psychologically essential.
- **Limit news consumption**: headline-doomscrolling is harmful. Check once or twice daily for useful information; filter the rest.
- **Physical activity**: non-negotiable. Walks, at-home exercise, movement.
- **Connection**: video calls, letters, safe outdoor meetups. Isolation is a greater long-term threat to most people than infection.

## Specific pandemic types — brief notes

- **Influenza variant (H5N1, etc.)**: standard respiratory protocols apply. Antivirals (oseltamivir) effective if started early. Vaccines usually available by wave 2.
- **SARS-type coronavirus**: transmission more aerosol-heavy. Ventilation and N95s matter disproportionately.
- **Hemorrhagic fever (Ebola, Marburg)**: bodily-fluid transmission. Barrier precautions, extreme care with medical waste. Unlikely to cause global pandemic but devastating locally.
- **Enteric (cholera, severe GI pathogen)**: water and food safety become primary. See sanitation references.
- **Antibiotic-resistant bacterial outbreak**: targeted antibiotic stockpile less useful; sanitation, wound care discipline, and avoidance of hospital-acquired infection matter.

## After the acute phase

Most pandemics transition from "emergency" to "endemic" over months to years. Watch for:

- **Waves and variants**: first wave is rarely the worst. Stay adaptive.
- **Long-term health effects**: both from the pathogen (post-COVID conditions) and from delayed medical care for other conditions. Schedule postponed appointments.
- **Healthcare system catch-up**: elective procedures resume gradually.
- **Economic and social effects**: often last much longer than the health emergency itself.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. Stock 10+ N95 or KN95 respirators per household member. Rotate annually — they do age.
2. Buy a pulse oximeter. Know what your household's baseline readings are.
3. Keep a thermometer (digital, two of them — they fail).
4. Know your primary care doctor's telehealth option.
5. Review vaccination status for everyone in the household.
6. If you have chronic medications, ask your doctor about 90-day refills during non-pandemic times.
7. Build the Corsi-Rosenthal box once. Test-run it. You'll have an intuition for the sound and placement.
8. Have a family conversation about which members are higher-risk and what your protocols will be if a pandemic emerges.
