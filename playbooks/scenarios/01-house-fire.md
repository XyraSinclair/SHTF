# House Fire

The most likely actual emergency you will face. A working smoke alarm plus two minutes of practiced response is the single highest-impact pair of lifesavers in this repo.

## If you only read one line

**Get out, stay out, call 911.** Do not go back in for anything. Houses burn faster than you think — flashover can occur 3–5 minutes after ignition. The time you have is shorter than the time you feel you have.

## First 60 seconds

The smoke alarm goes off:

1. **Wake everyone.** Shout, bang on doors, flip lights.
2. **Feel the bedroom door with the back of your hand.** Hot = do not open. Use the secondary exit (window). Cool = open slowly, be ready to slam it.
3. **Get low.** Clean air is 12–18 inches off the floor. Crawl, don't walk.
4. **Head for the nearest exit.** Do not detour for phones, pets, documents. Nothing.
5. **Close doors behind you** as you exit. Each closed door buys 10–20 minutes of fire progress.
6. **Meet at your predetermined outside meeting point.** (See [`../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md`](../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md).)
7. **Count heads.** If anyone is missing, tell arriving firefighters — do not re-enter.
8. **Call 911** from outside or from a neighbor's phone.

## If you cannot get out

- Go to a room with a window, preferably facing the street.
- Close the door. Stuff clothing or bedding at the base to slow smoke.
- Open the window, wave something bright, yell to anyone outside.
- Call 911, state your exact location in the house.
- Do not break the window unless you are getting out through it — breaking it lets in more smoke and feeds the fire.

## If clothing catches fire

**Stop, Drop, Roll.** Cover face with hands. Roll slowly, smothering the flames against the ground.

If someone else is on fire and you have a blanket, wrap them (rolling with them inside). If they are running, knock them down.

## If someone is burned

- Cool with **running water** (not ice) for 10–20 minutes.
- Do not apply butter, toothpaste, or home remedies.
- Do not pop blisters.
- Cover with clean, dry, non-stick material (a clean cotton sheet torn into pieces works; avoid towels that shed lint).
- Get medical attention for any burn larger than the palm, any burn on face/hands/feet/joints/genitals, and any burn on a child.
- Watch for shock: lay flat, legs up, warm, calm.

Citation: `survival-guides/FM4-25.11_First_Aid_Manual.pdf`, burn section.

## After the fire is out

The **24 hours after** a fire is when people often make their worst decisions. You are exhausted, in shock, homeless. What to do:

### Immediate (hours 0–6)

- Everyone gets checked by paramedics, even if you "feel fine." Smoke inhalation can appear hours later.
- **Do not re-enter the structure** until the fire department clears it. Structural collapse, hotspots, and toxic gases are real.
- Ask the fire department for:
  - A copy of the fire report.
  - The address of the nearest Red Cross field office (they handle emergency housing).
  - Whether your utilities were disconnected safely.

### First night

- Call your insurance company. Most homeowner's and renter's insurance covers emergency lodging.
- Red Cross provides hotel vouchers, clothing, food for disaster victims.
- Call your out-of-area contact. Activate the comms plan. (See [`../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md`](../tier-1-setup/05-family-comms-plan.md).)

### First week

- Retrieve documents from your encrypted USB or cloud backup. (See [`../tier-1-setup/04-cash-and-documents.md`](../tier-1-setup/04-cash-and-documents.md).)
- Replace: driver's license (DMV), insurance cards (call providers), credit cards (call issuers).
- Start a log of every expense. Insurance reimburses — if you documented it.
- Your property inventory video is now worth thousands. Provide it to the adjuster.
- Medications: your pharmacy can reissue from their records. Bring any ID you have.

### Longer term

- Total losses: your insurance pays actual cash value or replacement cost (read your policy). Negotiate; the first offer is rarely the final one.
- Smoke damage on spared structures: requires professional remediation; do not move back in until air quality is checked.
- Document everything for taxes; disaster losses may be deductible.
- Grief is normal. Losing a home is a bereavement event. Take it seriously.

## Prevention — the part that actually saves lives

### Smoke alarms

- One inside every bedroom.
- One outside each sleeping area.
- One on every level.
- **Test monthly. Replace batteries annually. Replace the alarm every 10 years.**
- Interconnected alarms (one triggers all) are vastly better than single-station. Either hardwired or 10-year battery models with wireless linking.

### Carbon monoxide alarms

- One on each level.
- Essential if you have any gas appliance, fireplace, attached garage, or use generators near the home.
- CO is odorless and has killed entire families in their sleep during power outages when they ran generators too close to the house.

### Kitchen fires

The #1 cause of home fires. If grease ignites:

- **Cover the pan with a metal lid.** Smothers it.
- Turn off the burner.
- Leave the lid on until completely cool.
- **Never use water** on a grease fire. It will explode into a fireball.
- Baking soda works on small grease fires; flour does not (and can explode).

Have a **Class K (kitchen) fire extinguisher** under the sink. ABC extinguishers work on many home fires but are less effective on grease.

### Electrical

- Do not overload outlets.
- Replace damaged cords immediately.
- Do not run extension cords under rugs or through doorways.
- Space heaters at least 3 feet from anything flammable, off when you leave or sleep.

### Heating

- Chimneys cleaned annually if you use the fireplace more than occasionally.
- Ash disposal: only in a metal can with a lid, kept outside away from the house for 48+ hours.
- Never use the oven for heat.

### Escape plan

- Draw a floor plan of your home. Mark two exits from every room.
- Practice the plan twice a year. Once in the dark.
- Kids should know: if you're trapped, go to a window; do not hide.
- Meeting point: a specific, visible location outside — mailbox, neighbor's porch, sidewalk across the street. Not "outside."
- Teach kids that firefighters in full gear look scary but are safe. Have them see the gear at a fire station visit if possible.

### Specific hazards

- **Lithium batteries**: e-bike, e-scooter, hoverboard batteries have caused a surge in house fires. Charge them in a fire-safe location, ideally not in bedrooms. Never leave a swelling, damaged, or hot battery in the house.
- **Candles**: #1 cause of fires during power outages. Flameless LED candles are equally ambient and don't kill.
- **Dryers**: clean the lint trap every load. Have the vent cleaned annually. Lint is exceptionally flammable.
- **Fireworks**: store outside in a metal container, away from the home.

## If you live in a multi-unit building

- Know the fire-escape route from every floor you might be on (yours, stairwells, a neighbor's).
- Know which stairwells are fire stairwells.
- Do not use the elevator.
- Close your apartment door as you leave — contains the fire.
- Stand-pipe fire hoses in hallways are for firefighter use, not residents.

## Pets

- Post a sticker on a front window indicating pets present and quantity. Fire services use these.
- In a fire: do not re-enter for pets. Firefighters will try.
- Pet carriers by the door, pre-set up: the pet goes in the carrier during slow-moving emergencies (e.g., wildfire warning), not at 2 AM when the smoke alarm goes off.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. Test every smoke alarm in your home tonight. Replace any that don't sound or are over 10 years old.
2. Install a CO alarm on every sleeping level if you don't have them.
3. Draw and post the escape plan (two exits from every room, meeting point).
4. Practice the plan with the household.
5. Buy a Class K kitchen extinguisher. Read the directions.
6. Check: is your video property inventory current? (See [`../tier-1-setup/04-cash-and-documents.md`](../tier-1-setup/04-cash-and-documents.md).)
