# Severe Weather

Hurricanes, tornadoes, blizzards, heat domes, ice storms. Each kills in different ways; the prep and response partially overlap.

## If you only read one line

**Weather kills most often through cascading infrastructure failure, not direct impact.** Power loss, heat/cold exposure, CO poisoning from improvised heating, and vehicle accidents during evacuation kill far more people than wind and water.

## Detection — know early

- **NOAA Weather Radio** (162.400–162.550 MHz) broadcasts continuous forecasts and emergency alerts. A $30 hand-crank radio + NOAA receiver is the single best dollar spent on weather awareness.
- **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)** on your phone — do not disable.
- **Local AM/FM radio** during active events.
- **The sky and the air pressure.** Learn the signs: greenish sky + wall cloud + calm after storm = tornado risk. Sudden temperature drop = cold front arriving. Distant lightning at night reveals storm distance — count seconds between flash and thunder, divide by 5 for miles.

Citation: `survival-guides/FM21-76_US_Army_Survival_Manual.pdf` weather chapter; NOAA frequencies in `radio/NOAA_Weather_Radio_Frequencies_West_Coast.txt`.

## Hurricane / tropical cyclone

### 72 hours before landfall

- Track it. Official source: National Hurricane Center (`nhc.noaa.gov`). Ignore social media predictions.
- Review [`../frameworks/stay-or-go.md`](../frameworks/stay-or-go.md). If mandatory evacuation is ordered AND you have >24 hours lead time, **go**.
- Fill vehicle fuel tanks. Lines form within hours.
- Fill bathtubs and every container with water.
- Charge all batteries. Buy ice if you can.

### 24 hours before

- Bring in or secure everything loose in the yard.
- Close and board windows if you have boards.
- Cash withdrawal, documents packed.
- Charge phones and battery packs.
- Last major cooking — perishable food goes first.

### During

- Stay away from windows.
- Shelter on the lowest interior floor (unless you are in a flood zone — then go up, not down).
- **Do not leave during the eye.** The eye is calm but second-half winds arrive from the opposite direction. Many deaths happen in the eye from people assuming it's over.

### After

- **Do not walk through floodwater.** It contains sewage, chemicals, sharp debris, and live electrical current from downed lines. 6 inches moves a person. 2 feet moves a car.
- **Do not touch downed power lines.** Treat all of them as live.
- Check on neighbors, especially elderly.
- Document damage before moving or cleaning anything (photos for insurance).
- **Generator safety**: outdoors only, 20+ feet from the house, away from open windows. Carbon monoxide is the hidden hurricane killer.

## Tornado

Tornadoes give you minutes, not hours.

### Warning issued

- **Interior room, lowest floor, no windows.** Basement best, interior bathroom or closet next, hallway last.
- Cover yourself with a mattress, blankets, or a bike helmet. Most tornado deaths are from flying debris.
- **Mobile homes are not safe.** Get out and go to the nearest sturdy structure, or lie in a ditch if no structure available.
- **A car is not safe.** If you are outside and cannot reach a building, a ditch below road grade is the least-bad option.

### If you are driving

- Do not try to outrun a tornado. They move unpredictably.
- Abandon the vehicle. Get into the lowest ground you can find.
- Do not shelter under an overpass — wind accelerates through the gap.

### After

- Be aware of gas leaks, downed lines, and unstable structures.
- Wear sturdy shoes. Tornado debris fields are razor-sharp.

## Blizzard / ice storm / severe cold

### Before the storm

- Fuel up the vehicle and top off heating fuel (propane, oil, wood).
- Have alternate heat: fireplace with wood, propane heater (indoor-rated), kerosene heater (with ventilation).
- **CO detector on every sleeping level.** Essential.
- Food and water as normal Tier 1 (see [`../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md`](../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md)).
- Insulate pipes; leave faucets dripping if temps drop below freezing for extended periods.

### During

- Close off unused rooms. Concentrate heat in one area.
- Dress in layers; change out of damp clothes immediately.
- Eat and drink normally — being cold burns calories fast.
- Check on vulnerable neighbors. Elderly alone in an unheated home is a leading winter-storm fatality.

### Hypothermia signs

Mild: shivering, cold extremities, slower thinking.

Moderate: violent shivering, confusion, poor coordination, slurred speech.

Severe: shivering stops, pupils dilated, very slow breathing, confused or unconscious.

Response: **move to warmth, remove wet clothing, wrap in dry insulating layers, give warm sugary fluid if conscious and able to swallow.** Do not give alcohol. Do not immerse in hot water. Do not rub extremities. Re-warming too fast can trigger cardiac arrest. See `survival-guides/FM21-76_US_Army_Survival_Manual.pdf` cold-weather section.

### Frostbite

- First-degree: skin is cold, red, tingly. Warm it.
- Second-degree: skin pale, waxy, numb. Blisters on re-warming. Serious.
- Third-degree: skin hard, blue or black. Medical emergency.

Re-warm in **warm water (100–108°F, 38–42°C), not hot.** Do not thaw if there is any chance of refreezing. Frozen-thawed-refrozen tissue is much worse than tissue that stays frozen longer.

### If your car is stranded

- **Stay with the car.** It is a landmark and shelter. People who leave cars in blizzards die at high rates.
- Run engine 10 min per hour to heat interior. **Crack a window** and make sure the exhaust pipe is clear of snow — otherwise carbon monoxide builds up and kills you.
- Tie bright cloth to the antenna. At night, inside light on.
- Move periodically to maintain circulation.
- Eat snow only if melted first and only for short durations — see [`../frameworks/myths-that-kill.md`](../frameworks/myths-that-kill.md).

## Heat dome / extreme heat

Heat is the deadliest weather category in the US. Most deaths are among elderly alone in un-air-conditioned homes.

### Prevention

- **Know who in your community is vulnerable.** Elderly, chronically ill, homeless, shift workers.
- **Cooling center locations**: most counties maintain these. Libraries, malls, community centers. Note the nearest.
- **Hydrate ahead.** Electrolytes matter — plain water in sustained heat without salt can cause hyponatremia.

### During

- Stay indoors in the coolest part of the house (basement, north-facing rooms).
- Close curtains on sunny sides.
- Fans alone are ineffective above ~95°F — they move hot air. An AC (even a small window unit) can save a life.
- Wet bandana on neck; wet T-shirt; cold washcloths at pulse points.
- Cool a hot person with water immersion (cool bath) or cold wet sheets. Do not use ice directly on skin.
- Kids and pets **never** left in a parked car. Interior temps can reach 130°F in minutes.

### Heat exhaustion vs. heat stroke

**Heat exhaustion**: heavy sweating, weak rapid pulse, dizziness, cramps, nausea, pale clammy skin. **Response**: move to cool, lie down, hydrate with electrolytes, wet skin, monitor.

**Heat stroke (life-threatening)**: body temp >103°F, hot dry (or wet) skin, confusion, fainting, seizures, loss of consciousness. **Response**: **call 911 immediately.** Cool aggressively — cold water immersion is the fastest, most effective method. Cold packs to neck, armpits, groin. Do not give fluids if unconscious.

## Ice storm

Ice storms do not look as dramatic as tornadoes, but they cause the most prolonged infrastructure damage. Power can be out for weeks.

- Do not drive. Black ice is invisible.
- Do not stand under trees or power lines. Ice-loaded branches break unpredictably.
- Treat extended outages as a grid-down scenario — see [`05-grid-down-extended.md`](05-grid-down-extended.md).
- Do not run a generator indoors or in a garage. CO poisoning spikes in every major ice storm.

## Flood (flash or sustained)

### Flash flood

- **Turn around, don't drown.** 6 inches of moving water knocks a person down. 2 feet moves a car.
- **Go up, not out.** Vertical evacuation is usually faster and safer than horizontal during a flash flood.
- Do not walk or drive through flowing water.

### Sustained flooding

- Move valuables to the highest floor before the water arrives.
- Turn off electricity at the main breaker if water will reach outlets.
- Do not return until authorities declare safe.
- Assume contamination. Any food or water touched by floodwater is discarded. Wells near flooded areas need testing before drinking.
- Mold starts within 24–48 hours on wet drywall. Aggressive drying (fans, dehumidifiers) matters.

## Lightning

- When you hear thunder, you are within strike range. "When thunder roars, go indoors."
- Inside: stay away from windows, plumbing, wired electronics. Cordless devices are fine.
- Outside with no shelter: spread out from other people (one strike can kill multiple people in contact). Squat in low ground. Do not lie flat.
- **A struck person does not carry charge**; CPR and first aid are safe to administer immediately.

## Dust storm / wildfire smoke

- Close windows, seal large gaps with a wet towel.
- Run HVAC on recirculate with a high-MERV filter (MERV 13+).
- A DIY "Corsi-Rosenthal box" (box fan + MERV filters) cleans the air of a room effectively for ~$80.
- N95 respirators are effective; cloth masks are not.
- Keep indoor activity low. Do not run fans that pull outside air in.
- Evacuate if Air Quality Index exceeds 500 for days and you have no filtration.

## Common across all weather scenarios

- **Power outage**: see [`05-grid-down-extended.md`](05-grid-down-extended.md).
- **Drinking water contamination**: see [`../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md`](../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md) and `survival-guides/Emergency_Water_Purification_Guide.pdf`.
- **Generator safety**: outdoors, 20+ ft from house, exhaust away from windows. CO kills more than fire.
- **Food safety**: 4-hour rule for unopened fridge, 48-hour rule for full freezer.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. Buy a NOAA Weather Radio. Program it. Test the alert.
2. Know your region's dominant weather threats (hurricanes vs. tornadoes vs. blizzards vs. wildfires).
3. Subscribe to your local county's emergency alert system (search "[your county] emergency alerts signup").
4. Identify your nearest cooling center and warming center.
5. Review `survival-guides/FM21-76_US_Army_Survival_Manual.pdf` weather and cold-injury chapters at least once.
