# Stranded or Lost

Car breakdown in a remote area. Lost on a trail. Boat adrift. Small plane down. The common thread: you are separated from normal support and rescue may take hours to days.

## If you only read one line

**Stop moving. Signal. Stay warm. Stay visible.** People who keep wandering are harder to find and more likely to injure themselves. Surviving 72 hours in one place almost always beats walking 20 miles in the wrong direction.

## Before anything else — STOP

A survival acronym that works:

- **S**top. Sit down. Panic loses people.
- **T**hink. Last known position. When were you expected? Who knows where you are?
- **O**bserve. What's around you — water, shelter, landmarks, wind direction?
- **P**lan. Signal-and-stay, or move (only with daylight and confidence)?

The first 10 minutes of calm thinking outperforms the next 2 hours of panicked movement.

## Your three priorities, in order

### 1. Shelter and body temperature

- Cold and wet kill faster than thirst.
- Hypothermia starts within hours, not days, in poor conditions.
- Your first physical priority is preventing further heat loss.

### 2. Signal

- Signal is your connection to rescue.
- See [`../frameworks/signalling-and-rescue.md`](../frameworks/signalling-and-rescue.md).
- Make yourself big, bright, and findable.

### 3. Water

- Most survival scenarios resolve before water becomes critical (under 72 hours in temperate conditions).
- If you will be out longer, water becomes more important than food.

### Food

- You can last weeks without food. Don't waste calories finding food in the short term.

## Specific scenarios

### Vehicle stranded in remote area

#### If you can drive out

- Go slow, keep the vehicle in control, don't power through snow or mud (makes it worse).
- If road is impassable: stop, don't abandon vehicle.

#### If vehicle is stuck

**Stay with the vehicle.** This is critical advice that people ignore at their peril. The vehicle is:

- Shelter from wind, sun, cold, rain.
- A landmark visible from the air and road.
- A source of heat (engine, interior with cracked windows).
- A container for all your supplies.

**Do not leave to "go for help"** unless:
- You have confirmed the road you'd walk to leads to help within 3–4 hours.
- You are fully equipped for the walk.
- You have left a note in the vehicle describing where you went, when, and what to do.

More people die walking away from stuck vehicles than freeze or starve in the vehicle.

#### Engine-running warming

- Run engine 10 minutes per hour for heat, not continuously (fuel preservation).
- **Crack a window.** Make sure exhaust pipe is clear of snow. CO buildup inside cars during blizzards has killed many.
- Headlights / blinkers during any running period conserve fuel compared to constant.

#### Visibility

- Tie bright cloth to antenna and/or door handle.
- Raise hood (universal distress signal).
- At night: interior dome light on (small draw, bright from distance).
- Three honks on the horn at intervals — signal distress.

#### Alternative heat

- Blankets and extra clothing from your car kit.
- Body heat from other people (huddle).
- Small candles in tin cans can raise interior temperature measurably (fire safety caveat; ventilation).

### Lost on a trail or in the wilderness

#### Before you panic

- When were you expected back? Your lateness triggers rescue within hours of an expected return — rescue starts searching before you know you're lost.
- Who has your itinerary?
- Where did you last know your position with certainty?

If you told someone where you were going and when you'd return, you have an asset. Most wilderness rescues resolve within 24–48 hours when itineraries are filed.

#### Stop and mark

- The moment you realize you're lost: stop. Do not "try to find the way back" — this is how people get truly lost.
- Mark your current position with a cairn, flag, or torn flagging tape. Any future search will find this.

#### Shelter priorities

- Wind shelter (natural windbreaks — trees, rocks, terrain).
- Off the ground (pine needles, leaves, clothing between you and soil — ground saps heat).
- Overhead cover against rain/snow (trees, improvised tarp).

#### Hypothermia prevention

- Stay dry. Wet cotton is an emergency — change to dry layers, even if they're less insulating.
- Move periodically to generate heat. Shivering is normal; violent shivering is a warning.
- Eat whatever you have — calories become heat.
- See [`../frameworks/triage.md`](../frameworks/triage.md) for hypothermia stages.

#### Water in the wilderness

- Running water above a spring is safest untreated, but treat when possible.
- Snow: melt before eating.
- Rain: catch in any container.
- Do not drink from stagnant pools, livestock areas, or downstream of settlements without treatment.
- Filter (0.2 micron) + disinfect (boil, tablets) for extended scenarios. See `survival-guides/Emergency_Water_Purification_Guide.pdf`.

#### Water in a desert

- Shade and reduced activity matter more than water-finding.
- Travel at night. Rest in shade during day.
- Do not drink urine, alcohol, seawater, or salt water. They dehydrate you faster.
- Morning dew on plastic sheeting or cloth yields usable water.
- If you find a dry wash: the lowest point may have moisture a foot or two down.

### Boat adrift

- **Stay with the boat.** Same principle as vehicle.
- **Issue mayday on VHF channel 16.** Repeat. Give position, number of persons, situation.
- **Life jackets on** always.
- **Activate EPIRB/PLB** if you have one.
- **Drogue anchor** (bucket, jacket) to slow drift.
- Ration water strictly. Do not drink seawater. Catching rainwater is essential for extended scenarios.

### Small plane down

- If you survive the landing: stay with the wreckage.
- Activate ELT if equipped.
- Do not attempt to return to the cabin if fire is present.
- ELT searches locate crashes within hours in populated areas, days in remote areas.
- Wreckage is visible to searchers; hiking away makes you invisible.

### Vehicle submerged or going into water

- **Unbuckle, open window (not door), exit.** Electric windows may fail after 60 seconds of submersion — act immediately.
- If water is already at window level: wait for pressures to equalize (cabin must flood), then open door.
- Assist children first, then swim to surface along with them.
- After surfacing: stay with vehicle if it's visible; swim to shore only if shore is close and clearly reachable.

### Lost at sea while swimming

- Float on back if possible.
- Activate any signal device.
- Do not waste energy swimming toward shore if shore is not visible — you will exhaust long before arrival.
- HELP position (Heat Escape Lessening Posture): tuck arms and legs, conserve heat.

## Navigation basics if you must move

See `survival-guides/FM3-25.26_Map_Reading_Land_Navigation.pdf`.

### Principles

- Travel in daylight only.
- Travel downhill and downstream in unfamiliar terrain — water almost always leads to human presence.
- Follow roads, rivers, power lines — linear features that connect to civilization.
- Keep the sun or a landmark at a consistent side of you for constant direction.

### Without a compass

- **Sun**: rises in the east, sets in the west. At solar noon, shadow points north in the Northern Hemisphere, south in Southern.
- **Stars (NH)**: Polaris (North Star) is nearly true north. Find via Big Dipper pointer stars.
- **Stars (SH)**: Southern Cross marks south, approximately.
- **Prevailing winds, moss on trees**: less reliable than TV claims, but can confirm general direction.
- **Analog watch**: point hour hand at sun; south is between hour hand and 12 (Northern Hemisphere, adjusted for daylight savings).

### Pacing

- Count pairs of steps to estimate distance. An average adult pair of steps ≈ 5 ft.
- Over 1 mile, expect to undercount by 5–10%.
- Uphill and uneven terrain makes pacing unreliable.

## Food priorities

- For scenarios under 72 hours: ignore food.
- For longer scenarios: edible plants, insects (most non-hairy insects are safe; avoid bright colors), small game if you can catch it.
- Universal edibility test (NWSS / FM21-76): if unfamiliar, test one small piece of plant — skin contact → lip → mouth chew without swallow → swallow, wait hours between steps. Skip this if you have any alternative food source.
- Avoid unknown mushrooms entirely. Animal-tested is not the same as human-tested.

## When to move vs. wait

**Move only if all of the following:**

1. You know the direction of help with confidence (not "I think").
2. You have daylight and weather for the duration of the walk plus margin.
3. Moving increases your visibility or brings you to resources, not deeper into wilderness.
4. You can leave a clear note or marker at your current location.
5. You have shelter and water for unexpected delays.

**Wait and signal if any of those are uncertain.** Rescue assumes you stay near your last known position.

## Key psychological hazards

- **Hopelessness on Day 2**: classic survival pattern. Most rescues happen day 2–4. Keep functional routines — eat, hydrate, signal-check, shelter-check.
- **Overestimating your energy**: exhausted people decide worse. Eat, sleep, wait.
- **Group conflict**: if with others, assign one decision maker, rotate if extended.
- **Survivors' guilt**: real after any group event. Address it; it doesn't resolve alone.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. **Always leave an itinerary.** Every wilderness trip: tell someone exactly where you're going, when you'll be back, when to call for help. This single habit triggers most successful searches.
2. **Car kit**: in the trunk, year-round — water, food (bars), blanket(s), flashlight, jumper cables, basic tools, small first aid, spare charger, high-visibility vest, small shovel, kitty litter or sand for traction, whistle.
3. **Wilderness day pack** for any hike: water + filter, food, layer, headlamp, knife, whistle, paper map, compass, lighter, space blanket.
4. **PLB or satellite messenger** for any serious backcountry: Garmin inReach or similar. Single most impactful piece of outdoor gear for cost.
5. **Learn to read a paper map and use a compass.** Practice before you need it.
6. **Emergency phrase with family**: "We have an overdue person on [trail / route]. Last contact [time]. Expected return [time]." This triggers the call to authorities.
