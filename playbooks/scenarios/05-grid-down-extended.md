# Grid-Down, Extended

Power is out. It's been out for days. Likely cascading from a weather event, cyberattack, equipment failure, or multi-utility incident. Most "grid-down" advice online is written for a fantasy version of this scenario; the real one is more boring and more survivable.

## If you only read one line

**The first 72 hours will be inconvenient. The first two weeks will be hard. Beyond that becomes a different kind of problem — community, not individual.** Plan for two weeks alone; plan for longer only by cooperating with neighbors.

## Phases

### Phase 1: Hours 0–12 (outage confirmed)

- Note the time of outage.
- Check in with neighbors — is it block-wide or larger? Utility trucks visible? Social media or news (via phone data or battery radio) active?
- Minimize fridge/freezer openings. Fridge: 4 hours safe. Full freezer: ~48 hours. Half-full freezer: ~24 hours.
- Charge devices from car, battery packs, solar panels while you still have daylight.
- Plan for the night: flashlights, headlamps staged, candles only with care (fire risk, especially with kids).
- Listen to a battery or hand-crank AM/FM radio. NOAA Weather Radio (162.400–162.550 MHz) for emergency broadcasts.
- Decide: stay or go ([`../frameworks/stay-or-go.md`](../frameworks/stay-or-go.md))? Usually stay.

### Phase 2: Day 1–3

- Eat perishables first. Cook on camp stove, grill, or fireplace — **outdoors only**. Generators and grills indoors kill via CO.
- Water: tap water usually continues for 24–72 hours depending on your utility's backup power. When pressure drops, start drinking from stored supplies.
- Medications requiring refrigeration (insulin, certain biologics): pack in cooler with ice. Pharmacies with generator power may be able to re-dispense or store meds.
- Information triage: broadcast radio is the authoritative source; social media is 10–40% wrong.
- Establish a household rhythm: dawn/dusk work schedule, meals at set times, kids' routines as close to normal as possible.

### Phase 3: Day 4–14

- Deep freezer contents now lost unless you've canned, dehydrated, or packed into a friend's working fridge.
- Sanitation becomes the primary health risk. Sewer often depends on electric pumps. If toilets back up: bucket toilet method (see below).
- Hygiene degrades. Hand washing discipline matters more than any single other factor in preventing diarrheal illness — which is the actual grid-down killer.
- Fuel discipline: generator run only when essential (refrigerator/freezer cycling, battery recharging, medical equipment). Siphon carefully from vehicles if needed.
- Community matters. Coordinate with neighbors on sharing fridges, generators, water, fuel. Isolated households fare worse.
- Check on vulnerable neighbors daily.

### Phase 4: 2+ weeks

- You are in a different scenario. Food stores dwindling. Cash economy dominant.
- If utility restoration timeline is unclear, consider moving to evacuation destination. See [`../frameworks/stay-or-go.md`](../frameworks/stay-or-go.md).
- Community mutual aid networks (faith organizations, neighbor groups) become essential. Participate.
- If you cannot get meds, seek medical care early — travel to a nearest operating clinic is better than waiting.

## Water

**Usually available for the first 1–3 days**, then pressure drops as utility backup systems fail or reservoirs drain. Plan accordingly.

- Draw water the moment you know the outage is serious. Fill tubs, containers, buckets.
- Water heater: 30–50 gallons of potable water. Shut off inlet first, open drain valve. See [`../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md`](../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md).
- Once pressure is lost, treat all tap water as suspect — bacterial infiltration through broken/depressurized mains is the primary risk.
- Purification: boil 1 min at sea level (3 min above 6,500 ft), or bleach 2 drops unscented per liter + 30 min, or filter to 0.2 microns + chemical disinfection.

## Sanitation

If sewer stops working (common after 1–3 days without power for pump stations):

### Twin-bucket system

- One 5-gallon bucket for solid waste, lined with heavy trash bag, sawdust or kitty litter added after each use.
- One 5-gallon bucket for urine (separated because mixing makes both smell worse and fills the solids bucket faster).
- Buckets changed every 1–2 days, solid bucket double-bagged and stored outside in a sealed container or deep-buried pit ≥200 ft from water.
- Toilet seat adapter (or a cut pool noodle) makes it bearable.

### Full reference

See `survival-guides/sanitation/Emergency_Toilet_Guidebook.pdf` and `survival-guides/FM21-10_Field_Hygiene_Sanitation.pdf`.

**Sanitation failure kills more people than the disaster that caused it.** Dysentery, cholera, and norovirus outbreaks in post-Katrina, post-Maria, and post-earthquake scenarios are documented. Hand washing, separating drinking water from waste, and keeping flies off both are not optional.

## Food

### What to eat in order

1. Perishables from the fridge (first 24–48h).
2. Freezer contents that thawed (next 24–48h — bacteria growth matters; if above 40°F for >2h, discard).
3. Canned goods, dry staples, shelf-stable.
4. Stored preps — see [`../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md`](../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md).

### Cooking

- Outdoors. Grill, camp stove, rocket stove, fire pit.
- Never indoors without massive ventilation.
- Kerosene, propane, and butane indoors with a window cracked is **not** enough ventilation — CO kills silently.

### Cold storage without power

- Chest freezer opened briefly keeps ice for 24–48h.
- Pack groceries tightly — less air, longer cold.
- Use ice blocks (frozen bottles of water before the outage). They release cold slowly and provide drinking water when melted.
- Deep freeze → ice chest with dry ice if available. Dry ice purchased at 3–5 lb per day of desired cooling per cooler.

## Power

### Priority loads

In order of what to keep running if fuel is limited:

1. **Medical** — CPAP, oxygen concentrator, insulin pump chargers, dialysis equipment.
2. **Communication** — phone charging, radios.
3. **Refrigeration** — only while food is still worth saving.
4. **Lights at night** — LED headlamps and lanterns sip power.
5. **Heat/cool** — only in extreme weather for vulnerable people.
6. Everything else is luxury.

### Generators

- Inverter generators (Honda EU2200i, Predator 3500i, etc.) are quieter, more fuel-efficient, and produce cleaner power for electronics.
- Conventional gas generators need stabilizer in fuel; cycle the gas annually.
- **NEVER run indoors or in garages.** CO is a silent killer in every major blackout. Minimum 20 ft from any window or door, exhaust pointing away.
- Fuel: ~1 gallon gasoline per hour under moderate load for a 2kW generator. Plan fuel supply accordingly.
- Tri-fuel (gasoline/propane/natural gas) generators are versatile. Propane stores indefinitely without degradation.

### Solar + battery

- Small solar panels + portable power stations (EcoFlow, Goal Zero, Jackery) are silent, fuel-free, and increasingly affordable.
- A 500–1000 Wh battery + 100W panel covers phone charging, lighting, and modest fridge cycling.
- Not a replacement for generators in extended outages but a valuable supplement.

### Solar-recharge priorities

If you have limited solar capacity: charge batteries and battery packs first, then run dedicated fridges if compatible, then lights.

## Communications

- Cell towers have battery backup for ~4–8 hours; after that, spotty.
- Text messages route when voice doesn't; use them.
- AM/FM radio from battery or hand-crank is the most reliable inbound channel during outages.
- NOAA Weather Radio for emergency information.
- Ham / GMRS radio lets you communicate with other operators when cell is down. See `radio/UV-5R_Quick_Reference_Card.pdf`.
- Internet: cellular data sometimes works, sometimes doesn't. Starlink dishes with battery backup are increasingly common in disaster response.

See [`../cards/radio-frequencies.md`](../cards/radio-frequencies.md) for emergency frequencies.

## Heat and cold

### Heating without utility power

- Wood stove / fireplace (assuming stocked wood and clean chimney).
- Propane/kerosene indoor-rated heaters (Mr. Heater Buddy, etc.) with ventilation.
- **No gas stoves, ovens, or grills for heating.** CO.
- Concentrate heat in one room. Close doors to others. Body heat adds up.

### Cooling without utility power

- Harder than heating. Cars with A/C can be a temporary cooling option (outdoors, in shade, engine idling).
- Shade, airflow, wet cloths on pulse points.
- Cooling centers at libraries, malls, shelters. Move vulnerable people there.

## Financial

- Cash is king. ATMs and card readers are down across entire regions in major outages. See [`../tier-1-setup/04-cash-and-documents.md`](../tier-1-setup/04-cash-and-documents.md).
- Businesses that are open often accept cash only.
- Do not drive looking for working ATMs on day 1–2 — fuel, time, and risk.

## Transportation

- Keep your tank above half full through the outage.
- Fuel stations run out of fuel before utility restoration in many outages (their own pumps need power; fuel trucks prioritize emergency services).
- **Do not siphon gasoline from strangers' vehicles.** It is theft and dangerous.
- Electric vehicles are charging-limited; know the nearest charging station with generator backup.

## Medical continuity

- Prescription medications: most can be extended in a crisis. If you run out, call the pharmacy — many will dispense emergency refills.
- Insulin: store at 36–46°F. Coolers with ice work. Unrefrigerated insulin is usable for ~28 days per manufacturer guidelines.
- Dialysis: confirm your clinic's backup plan. Some have generator backup; some do not.
- CPAP and oxygen: know run times from backup battery; check generator power availability.

## Before it happens, do this once

1. Buy flashlights and headlamps for every household member + spare batteries. Stage by beds and doors.
2. Stock 14 days of food and water per [`../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md`](../tier-1-setup/03-water-food-stockpile.md).
3. Buy a hand-crank or battery AM/FM/NOAA radio (~$30).
4. Charge all phones nightly. Keep a 10,000+ mAh battery pack charged, always.
5. Know where your water heater drain valve is. Practice using it once.
6. Know how to shut off gas and water main valves. Label them.
7. If you have space and fuel storage options: a small inverter generator and 10–20 gallons of rotated gasoline is a significant quality-of-life upgrade for any outage. Not essential; a nice-to-have.
8. Meet at least three neighbors. Know who has kids, elderly family, medical equipment. You will be each other's first responders.
