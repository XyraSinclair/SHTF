# Household Recovery

## If you only read one line
Treat recovery as a temporary job, divide the work, protect the kids and elders, and defer life-changing decisions made from grief or exhaustion.
The house may be damaged.
The household is also damaged.
Recovery work can consume 20-40 hours a week during the hardest phases.
Someone becomes the claims clerk, scheduler, document keeper, negotiator, driver, cook, school liaison, and emotional shock absorber.
That load breaks people if it stays invisible.
Make the work visible.
Divide it.
Rotate it.

## The new full-time job
Post-disaster recovery is work.
Name it.
Put it on the calendar.
Set office hours.
Example:
- 8:30-10:30 calls and forms
- 10:30-11:00 receipts and binder
- 4:00-4:30 family update
- after dinner no claims talk unless urgent
One adult should lead at a time.
The lead:
- keeps the binder current
- owns the calendar
- tracks deadlines
- assigns tasks
- confirms decisions in writing
- protects the household from duplicate calls
Rotate the lead every two weeks if possible.
If only one adult is available, assign outside helpers to specific jobs.
Do not say "help us."
Say:
- scan these receipts
- drive Dad to the clinic
- watch the kids Tuesday
- compare these three contractor bids
- sit with me while I call insurance
- pick up prescriptions
- take the dog to boarding
Specific help gets done.
Vague help fades.

## Kids
Children need truth, routine, and steady adults.
They do not need perfect explanations.
Common reactions:
- regression
- clinginess
- nightmares
- school refusal
- new fears
- anger
- stomachaches
- headaches
- repeated questions
- acting like nothing happened
- trying to parent the parent
Predictable routine matters more than perfect routine.
Keep:
- wake time
- meals
- school contact
- bedtime
- one familiar object
- one adult check-in
Use simple language:
- "The fire is out."
- "We cannot sleep at home tonight."
- "Adults are working on the next place."
- "You will go to school if the school is open."
- "You can ask again later."
Do not say:
- "Everything is fine."
- "Do not cry."
- "Be the man of the house."
- "You need to be strong for Mom."
- "We will be home by Friday" unless you know.
Use [`../cards/psychological-first-aid.md`](../cards/psychological-first-aid.md).
Tell school:
- what happened
- where the child is staying
- what signs to watch for
- whether transportation changed
- who may pick up the child
- whether McKinney-Vento support may apply

## Elderly family
Older adults can decline fast after displacement.
Watch for:
- confusion in new environments
- falls
- missed medications
- lost glasses
- lost hearing aids
- lost dentures
- lost mobility equipment
- oxygen disruption
- dialysis disruption
- dehydration
- pressure sores
- sleep reversal
- delirium after infection or stress
- caregiver collapse
Rebuild the care network fast.
Call:
- primary-care clinician
- pharmacy
- home-health agency
- durable medical equipment supplier
- oxygen supplier
- dialysis center
- Area Agency on Aging
- insurance care manager
- utility medical baseline program
Write a one-page elder sheet:
- diagnoses
- medications
- allergies
- equipment
- baseline mobility
- baseline cognition
- emergency contacts
- doctors
- pharmacy
- insurance
- hearing, vision, dental needs
- diet limits
Put it in the binder.
Tape a copy inside the temporary housing door if appropriate.

## Marriage and partnership
Recovery stresses partnerships because it creates constant decisions under loss.
Common conflict loops:
- one person wants action, the other wants pause
- one reads every detail, the other avoids mail
- one wants to rebuild, the other wants to move
- one spends for comfort, the other freezes money
- one blames, the other shuts down
- both are sleep deprived
Set rules before the fight:
- no major decisions after 9 PM
- no contractor signatures without both adults or a named backup
- no disaster talk in bed
- one weekly money meeting
- one weekly no-disaster hour
- one shared written task list
- one person speaks to each agency unless delegated
Use a weekly "no disaster talk" hour.
Put it on the calendar.
Take a walk.
Eat a meal.
Watch something harmless.
Sit in silence.
The point is to remind the nervous system that the disaster is not every minute.
If conflict is escalating, a couples therapist helps by:
- stopping blame cycles
- translating different coping styles
- setting decision rules
- protecting children from adult conflict
- making repair after hard conversations
If there is violence, threats, coercive control, or fear at home, use domestic-violence resources and a safety plan.
Do not treat violence as normal disaster stress.

## Divide the labor
Use named roles:
- insurance lead
- FEMA lead
- contractor lead
- household lead
- money lead
- school lead
- elder-care lead
- pet lead
One person can hold more than one role.
One role should not hold every role.
Each lead writes:
- next deadline
- next call
- documents needed
- decision needed
- money at stake
- blocker
Use one shared place:
- recovery binder
- shared cloud folder
- whiteboard
- notebook
- spreadsheet
Do not run recovery through scattered text threads.
Texts are fine for quick updates.
Decisions go in the shared place.

## Pet loss grief
Pet loss after a disaster is real grief.
Do not minimize it.
For many children and adults, the pet was the last stable attachment to the old home.
Small memorials help:
- print one photo
- keep a collar or tag
- plant something
- write the pet's name on a stone
- let children draw or write
- tell the story once without rushing
Do not tell a child "we will get another one" as the main comfort.
Replacement is not grief care.

## Anniversary reactions
The one-year mark often reopens the event.
It can start weeks early.
Signs:
- irritability
- sleep changes
- dread
- replaying details
- avoiding the date
- checking weather or news
- sudden grief
- conflict over whether to mark the day
Plan something deliberate.
Options:
- quiet family meal
- visit the rebuilt home
- avoid the site
- thank helpers
- attend faith service
- volunteer
- finish one binder task
- memorialize what was lost
- take the day off if possible
- schedule therapy that week
Tell children what the plan is.
Tell school if the date may be hard.
Tell work if you need flexibility.

## Decisions to defer for a year
When possible, defer major life decisions made from adrenaline, grief, fear, or exhaustion.
Defer:
- divorce
- permanent move
- major job change
- quitting school
- buying a replacement home in panic
- taking on major new debt
- having a new baby to "start over"
- cutting off family permanently
- selling land or heirlooms
Some decisions cannot wait.
Unsafe housing, violence, immigration deadlines, custody orders, medical needs, and income loss may force action.
When action is forced, slow it down:
- write the reason
- list options
- sleep before signing if possible
- get one trusted outside read
- check money impact
- check legal deadline
- check child or elder impact
Adrenaline is bad decision fuel.
Use time as medicine when you can.

## 5 most important actions

1. Name one recovery lead and rotate that role every two weeks if possible.
2. Divide insurance, FEMA, contractor, money, school, elder, and pet tasks by role.
3. Keep children on predictable routines and tell schools about the displacement.
4. Rebuild elder care, medication, equipment, and appointment systems first.
5. Schedule one weekly no-disaster hour and one weekly recovery meeting.

## Common mistakes

- Letting the most competent person carry every call, form, and decision until collapse.
- Treating children's regression as bad behavior instead of disaster stress.
- Missing elder decline because everyone is focused on the damaged house.
- Making major family decisions during the worst sleep and money period.
- Running decisions through scattered text threads with no single record.
- Minimizing pet loss because people also lost property.

## Before it happens, do this once
Make a household role list, elder care sheet, child pickup list, pet plan, and trusted-helper list before any disaster.
