# Contractor Vetting After a Disaster

## If you only read one line
Verify the license, verify insurance with the carrier, get three written bids for the same scope, and do not sign over insurance rights.
After a disaster, the honest contractors are overloaded.
The scammers arrive fast.
The rushed contract is often the second disaster.
Slow down the signature.
Speed up the verification.

## Post-disaster fraud surge
Disaster repair fraud has patterns.
Learn the script.
Common pitches:
- "We are in the neighborhood."
- "Your insurance will cover all of it."
- "We can start today if you sign now."
- "The price is only good until tonight."
- "We need cash for materials."
- "You do not need permits."
- "We work directly with FEMA."
- "Sign this so we can talk to your insurer."
- "Your roof is unsafe; I can show you photos."
Red flags:
- door-to-door pressure
- out-of-state plates with no local license
- no permanent business address
- no written scope
- no permit plan
- no license number
- no insurance certificate sent by the carrier
- demand for full payment up front
- request for cash
- refusal to provide references
- offer to pay your insurance deductible
- offer to inflate the estimate
- Assignment of Benefits language
- blank spaces in the contract
If you feel rushed, stop.
Real contractors can wait long enough for verification.

## Finding legitimate contractors
Start with sources that leave a trail:
- your state contractor licensing board
- local building department
- your insurance agent, not just the adjuster
- local independent insurance agents
- neighbors with completed repairs
- trade associations such as NARI
- BBB profile as a supplement, not proof
- manufacturer certified-installer directories for roofing, HVAC, windows, and mitigation systems
Angi, Yelp, social media, and ads can produce leads.
They do not verify license, insurance, liens, workmanship, or contract terms for you.
Use them only as a starting list.
Ask the local building department:
- whether permits are required
- whether the contractor has pulled permits locally
- whether emergency permit rules apply
- whether inspections are delayed after the disaster
- whether there are known fraud warnings

## License verification
Do not accept a photocopy as proof.
Call or search the state licensing board yourself.
Verify:
- exact business name
- license number
- license type
- license status
- expiration date
- qualifying individual if listed
- disciplinary actions
- bond status if your state uses bonds
- complaint history if available
Make sure the license covers the work.
A handyman license may not cover structural work.
A roofing license may not cover electrical.
A mold-remediation registration may not cover reconstruction.
Save a screenshot or PDF of the license lookup in the binder.

## Insurance verification
Ask for:
- general liability certificate
- workers' compensation certificate
- auto liability if vehicles will be on site
- umbrella policy if the job is large
Then call the insurance carrier or agent listed on the certificate.
Ask:
- "Is this policy active today?"
- "Does it cover this type of work?"
- "What are the limits?"
- "Is workers' compensation active for employees?"
- "Will you send a certificate directly to me?"
- "Can my property address be listed as certificate holder?"
Do not accept:
- certificate from the contractor only
- expired certificate
- name mismatch
- certificate for a different company
- "we are exempt" without checking state rules
If an uninsured worker is hurt on your property, you can be dragged into the mess.

## The three-bid rule
Get three written bids.
Use the same written scope for each.
Do not ask one contractor for a roof, one for a roof plus decking, and one for a full exterior rebuild, then compare totals.
Write a scope sheet:
- rooms or areas
- visible damage
- known hidden-damage concerns
- materials grade
- code items
- permits
- debris removal
- temporary protection
- start date
- completion target
- exclusions
Give the same scope sheet to every bidder.
Ask each bidder to separate:
- labor
- materials
- permits
- debris removal
- remediation
- subcontractors
- allowances
- contingency
- change-order rates
Low bid is not automatically best.
A bid that omits permits, disposal, code items, or hidden damage may become the highest bid later.

## References
Ask for four references:
- two recent post-disaster jobs
- two older jobs at least one year old
Call them.
Ask:
- Did the contractor finish?
- Did the final price match the change orders?
- Were permits pulled and closed?
- Were inspections passed?
- Were subcontractors paid?
- Did any lien notices arrive?
- Was the site left safe each day?
- Did workers show up when promised?
- Did the contractor answer after final payment?
- Would you hire them again for the same job?
Ask to see one completed job if the owner agrees.
Do not rely on photos alone.
Photos do not show permit closure, lien problems, or months-later leaks.

## The contract
No handshake repairs except tiny emergency stabilization.
The contract should include:
- legal business name
- license number
- physical address
- phone and email
- property address
- detailed scope of work
- materials by brand, model, grade, color, or allowance
- permits and who pulls them
- total cost
- payment schedule
- start date
- completion date
- change-order process
- cleanup and debris removal
- site safety
- warranty terms
- insurance requirements
- subcontractor list if known
- dispute process
- cancellation rights
- lien-waiver requirements
- signature blocks
Avoid:
- blank spaces
- "as needed" work with no rates
- "insurance proceeds" as the price
- open-ended material allowances
- no completion date
- no change-order process
- no lien-waiver requirement

### Payment schedule
Tie payments to completed phases.
Example:
- deposit after contract signing
- payment after materials delivered
- payment after rough work passes inspection
- payment after substantial completion
- final payment after punch list, final inspection, and lien waivers
Never pay more than 10-20% up front unless state law sets a lower cap or a lender/insurer draw process controls the funds.
Never pay cash.
Use checks, card, bank bill pay, or another traceable method.
Write the job address and invoice number on every payment.

### Completion date and delay terms
Put a completion date in writing.
For large jobs, ask about liquidated damages.
That means a set daily amount if delay is the contractor's fault.
State law controls enforceability.
Even if you do not use liquidated damages, a dated schedule forces a real conversation.

### Arbitration vs. court
Many contracts require arbitration.
Arbitration can be faster.
It can also limit discovery, appeal rights, and public pressure.
Court can be slower.
It may give stronger discovery and lien remedies.
Read the dispute clause before signing.
A construction attorney reviews the contract, lien language, payment schedule, arbitration clause, and state-required notices before you sign.
That is the useful reason to hire one.

## Lien waivers
If subcontractors or suppliers are not paid, they may file a lien against your property even if you paid the general contractor.
Use lien waivers.
Ask for:
- conditional lien waiver with each progress payment
- unconditional lien waiver after payment clears
- final lien waiver before final payment
- list of major subs and suppliers
Confirm state forms and rules.
Keep every waiver in the binder.
Do not make final payment until:
- work is complete
- punch list is complete
- final inspection passed if required
- warranty documents received
- lien waivers received

## Assignment of Benefits
Assignment of Benefits, or AOB, can transfer insurance claim rights to a contractor or vendor.
Do not sign AOB language casually.
It may let the contractor:
- control claim payments
- negotiate directly with the insurer
- sue the insurer in your name or their own
- collect money before you understand the bill
- create disputes over what work was authorized
Look for phrases like:
- assignment of benefits
- direction to pay
- power of attorney
- authorization to negotiate
- proceeds assigned
- all rights under policy
Some limited direct-pay authorizations are normal.
Broad assignment of policy rights is different.
If you do not understand it, do not sign it at the door.
Ask your insurer, state insurance department, or a lawyer who reviews contractor documents.

## Change orders
Hidden damage is real.
Blank checks are not.
Every change order should state:
- reason for change
- added work
- removed work
- added cost
- credit
- time added
- photos if hidden damage
- signatures before work proceeds
Emergency exception:
If immediate work prevents further damage or makes the site safe, document it by text or email the same day and formalize it as soon as possible.

## 5 most important actions

1. Verify the contractor license through the state board before signing.
2. Verify insurance by calling the carrier, not by trusting a certificate.
3. Get three bids using the same written scope of work.
4. Use a written contract with phase payments, permits, change orders, and lien waivers.
5. Refuse broad Assignment of Benefits language unless a lawyer explains the exact effect.

## Common mistakes

- Hiring the first door-to-door crew because they can start tomorrow.
- Comparing three bids that cover three different scopes.
- Paying cash or large deposits before materials or work appear.
- Skipping lien waivers and discovering unpaid subcontractors later.
- Letting a contractor promise what insurance will cover.
- Signing emergency cleanup paperwork with AOB language hidden inside.

## Before it happens, do this once
Bookmark your state contractor licensing lookup, state insurance department, local building department, and two trusted local trade referrals in the recovery binder.
