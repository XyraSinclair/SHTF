# Insurance Claims After a Disaster

## If you only read one line
Call your insurer early, document before cleanup, and do not throw away damaged property until the adjuster or the insurer tells you in writing.
Insurance is a contract.
Your job is to prove a covered loss.
The insurer's job is to adjust the claim under the policy.
Do not confuse sympathy with coverage.
Do not confuse the first payment with the final payment.

## The first call
Call as soon as everyone is safe.
Use the claims number on your policy or insurer app.
If the phone system is jammed, file online and screenshot the confirmation.
Start the claims diary before you call.
Record:
- date
- time
- phone number dialed
- person or operator ID
- claim number
- exact words they use for next steps
- documents they request
- deadline they give
Say:
- "I am reporting a loss."
- "The date of loss is ____."
- "The property address is ____."
- "People are safe / injured / displaced."
- "The home is habitable / not habitable / unknown."
- "There is possible structural damage / water damage / smoke damage / contents loss."
- "I need instructions on temporary repairs and loss-of-use coverage."
- "Please send all coverage and claim instructions in writing."
Do not say:
- "It was my fault."
- "It is only cosmetic."
- "Everything is a total loss."
- "We can stay with family for free."
- "I accept that amount."
- "I do not need an adjuster."
- "The contractor says this is covered."
You can say "I do not know yet."
That is often the most accurate answer.

## Secure the property without destroying evidence
Prevent further damage.
Most policies require reasonable steps to protect the property after a loss.
Do:
- tarp an open roof if safe
- board broken windows
- shut off water if pipes burst
- move undamaged items away from active water
- save receipts for tarps, plywood, locks, fans, and storage
Do not:
- start demolition before inspection unless safety requires it
- discard soaked carpet, furniture, appliances, or clothing without photos and approval
- sign a contractor's emergency work order you have not read
- let a cleanup company remove everything without an inventory
If the fire department, building inspector, or utility orders removal for safety, photograph first and keep the written order.

## Documenting everything
Assume you will forget details.
You will.
Use your phone while the scene is still fresh.

### Photo technique
Take wide shots first.
Then medium shots.
Then close shots.
For each room:
- stand in the doorway
- photograph all four walls
- photograph the ceiling
- photograph the floor
- photograph closets and cabinets
- photograph serial-number plates
- photograph labels on appliances and tools
Use a ruler, coin, or hand for scale only after the wide shot.
Turn on date and location metadata if you can.
If metadata is off, take one photo of a paper with the date and address.

### Video technique
Walk slowly.
Speak plainly.
Say:
- room name
- what happened
- what is damaged
- what still works
- what smells like smoke, sewage, or fuel
- what you cannot safely access
Do not narrate guesses as fact.
Say "I see water line at about knee height" instead of "the foundation is ruined."

### Home inventory after the fact
Build the inventory room by room.
Use:
- old photos
- holiday photos
- real estate listing photos
- receipts
- credit card statements
- email order confirmations
- app purchase history
- warranty registrations
- manuals
- repair records
- neighbors' photos
For each item, list:
- item
- brand
- model
- approximate age
- purchase place
- replacement cost
- condition before loss
- photo or proof
Do the high-value rooms first:
- kitchen
- garage
- tools
- electronics
- office
- medical equipment
- clothing closets

## Do not throw anything out before the adjuster
Damaged property is evidence.
If it stinks, leaks, or grows mold, move it outside if safe.
Photograph it in place first.
Then photograph it after moving.
Keep samples when full items cannot stay:
- a square of damaged flooring
- a cabinet door
- appliance serial-number tag
- carpet pad sample
- wallpaper sample
- roof shingle
Bag and label samples:

```text
Item:
Room:
Date removed:
Reason removed:
Photos stored at:
```

## ACV vs. RCV
ACV means actual cash value.
It usually subtracts depreciation for age and wear.
RCV means replacement cost value.
It pays the cost to repair or replace with like kind and quality, subject to the policy.
Many replacement-cost policies pay in two steps:
- first ACV payment
- recoverable depreciation after you replace or repair and submit receipts
Do not spend the ACV check on unrelated costs if you need the RCV later.
Ask:
- "Is this ACV or RCV?"
- "Is depreciation recoverable?"
- "What receipts do you need to release recoverable depreciation?"
- "What is the deadline to complete replacement?"
- "What items have special limits?"
Special limits often apply to:
- jewelry
- cash
- firearms
- collectibles
- tools used for business
- art
- electronics

## Loss of Use / Additional Living Expenses
Loss of Use or Additional Living Expenses is the policy bucket that pays extra costs when a covered loss makes the home unfit to live in.
Read the declarations page for the limit.
Ask the insurer:
- "Is ALE open on this claim?"
- "What is my limit?"
- "Do you pay advances?"
- "Do you require pre-approval for hotels or rentals?"
- "Do restaurant meals need itemized receipts?"
- "How do you calculate extra food cost over my normal budget?"
- "Are pet boarding, storage, laundry, mileage, and parking covered?"
Keep receipts for:
- hotel
- rent
- deposits
- restaurant meals
- groceries above normal
- laundry
- parking
- tolls
- storage
- mileage
- pet boarding
- moving supplies
Do not say you have free housing if it is not stable.
If you are sleeping on a couch for three nights, say that.
If you need a rental near school, work, medical care, or disability access, say that.

## Flood and earthquake exclusions
Most homeowners and renters policies do not cover flood damage.
Flood insurance is usually separate, often through the National Flood Insurance Program.
Most standard homeowners policies do not cover earthquake damage.
Earthquake insurance is separate. In California, CEA policies are sold through participating residential insurers.
Ask what caused each damage line:
- wind-driven rain
- rising floodwater
- sewer backup
- broken pipe
- storm surge
- earth movement
- fire following earthquake
Coverage can depend on the cause.
Do not let a contractor assign the cause.
Do not guess.

## Working with adjusters
There may be more than one adjuster:
- company adjuster
- independent adjuster hired by insurer
- flood adjuster
- contents adjuster
- large-loss adjuster
- public adjuster you hire
Before the inspection:
- print or save your policy declarations
- list damaged rooms
- list urgent safety issues
- collect photos
- collect receipts
- mark inaccessible areas
- prepare questions
During the inspection:
- walk with the adjuster if allowed
- point out every area
- ask what they did not inspect
- ask what documents they need
- ask when to expect the estimate
- take your own notes
After the inspection:
- request the adjuster's estimate
- compare line by line against contractor bids
- check quantities
- check room dimensions
- check code items
- check contents categories
- check sales tax, debris removal, permits, and overhead/profit where applicable
Disagree in writing.
Attach evidence.
Do not argue only by phone.

## Public adjusters
A public adjuster works for you, not the insurance company.
They inspect, estimate, document, and negotiate the property claim.
They are usually paid a percentage of the insurance settlement.
Ten percent is common in some markets, but state caps and disaster rules vary.
Consider hiring one when:
- the loss is large
- the home is a total or near-total loss
- the insurer estimate misses major damage
- you cannot manage the paperwork
- there is business property, code upgrade, smoke, or complex water damage
- you have repeated delays or rotating adjusters
Do not hire one when:
- the claim is small
- the fee would eat the useful recovery money
- the adjuster is not licensed in your state
- the contract gives them control over checks beyond their fee
- the contract blocks you from direct communication with the insurer
Before signing:
- verify the license with the state insurance department
- read the cancellation period
- confirm the fee
- confirm what claim money the fee applies to
- confirm who receives checks
- keep a copy of the signed contract

## Denials and bad-faith practices
A denial is not the end.
Read the denial letter slowly.
Find:
- policy provision cited
- facts the insurer relied on
- documents they say are missing
- deadline for response
- appeal or appraisal rights
- complaint options
Write a response with:
- claim number
- date of loss
- exact issue
- why the denial is wrong or incomplete
- photos
- contractor estimate
- engineer report if needed
- receipts
- witness statement if useful
Bad-faith rules are state law.
Warning signs:
- no written reason for denial
- repeated lost documents
- unexplained delay
- low estimate with missing rooms
- pressure to accept before full damage is known
- refusal to identify policy language
- claim closed while requested documents are pending
File a complaint with your state insurance department when the insurer will not correct the problem.
An insurance attorney does three things:
- reads the policy and state claim-handling rules
- writes a demand tied to law and evidence
- files suit if the insurer violates the contract or state law
Hire one only when the claim size, delay, or denial justifies that cost.

## 5 most important actions

1. Open the claim and write the claim number in the recovery binder.
2. Photograph and video every damaged area before cleanup.
3. Save damaged property or labeled samples until released in writing.
4. Separate ACV money, RCV money, and ALE receipts in the binder.
5. Dispute missing or wrong estimate lines in writing with evidence attached.

## Common mistakes

- Saying "I accept" before reading the estimate and payment letter.
- Throwing away contents because they smell bad, then having no proof.
- Letting a contractor speak for coverage instead of reading the policy.
- Missing recoverable depreciation deadlines after receiving the ACV check.
- Forgetting ALE receipts for food, laundry, mileage, parking, and pet boarding.
- Hiring an unlicensed public adjuster from a disaster-zone sales pitch.

## Before it happens, do this once
Put your declarations pages, agent number, insurer claims number, and a home inventory video in the recovery binder and cloud folder.
