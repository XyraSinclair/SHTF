# Mental Health Timeline After a Disaster

## If you only read one line
Expect the crash after the emergency phase, keep sleep and connection on the schedule, and call 1-800-985-5990 or 988 if safety becomes a concern.
Disaster stress changes over time.
Feeling fine in week one does not prove you are fine.
Falling apart at month three does not prove you are weak.
SAMHSA describes common disaster phases:
- Heroic
- Honeymoon
- Disillusionment
- Reconstruction
The timing varies.
The pattern matters.

## Week 1: Heroic and Honeymoon
This is the adrenaline phase.
People rescue, clean, carry, call, cook, and make decisions with very little sleep.
Common signs:
- intense energy
- narrow focus
- little appetite
- little sleep
- feeling useful
- feeling numb
- survivor's high
- closeness with neighbors
- constant phone checking
- repeated retelling
This phase can help you function.
It can also hide injury and grief.
Do:
- sleep in blocks when you can
- eat simple food on a schedule
- drink water
- limit alcohol
- assign tasks
- write decisions down
- keep kids near predictable adults
- keep medications on schedule
- take photos and notes before memory blurs
Do not:
- make permanent decisions at 2 AM
- tell yourself sleep is optional
- promise children everything will be back to normal soon
- shame someone who is frozen or crying
- treat numbness as proof nothing happened
Psychological first aid here means:
- safety
- calm
- practical help
- connection
- hope tied to real next steps
Use [`../cards/psychological-first-aid.md`](../cards/psychological-first-aid.md).

## Month 1: Disillusionment begins
The helpers leave.
The forms stay.
The insurer asks for more.
FEMA sends a confusing letter.
The hotel gets old.
School is disrupted.
Work expects you back.
This is where anger often starts.
Common signs:
- fatigue
- irritability
- short temper
- bureaucratic rage
- sadness after activity stops
- trouble concentrating
- marital strain
- money panic
- intrusive memories
- nightmares
- guilt
- conflict with relatives
- resentment toward people who "moved on"
Do:
- set recovery office hours
- keep one shared task list
- schedule one no-disaster conversation each day
- get outside in daylight
- keep kids in school or routine if possible
- say no to extra obligations
- reduce major decisions
- keep medical and mental-health medications stable
Use the Disaster Distress Helpline if distress is high:

```text
1-800-985-5990
```
Use 988 for suicidal thoughts, crisis, or immediate safety concern:

```text
988
```

## Months 3-6: the long grind
This is often harder than the first month.
The public story is over.
Your claim is not.
Your house may still be open walls.
Your savings may be gone.
Relationships show strain.
Common signs:
- exhaustion
- hopelessness
- conflict over money
- conflict over contractors
- avoidance of mail
- sleep loss
- substance use
- emotional numbness
- rage at small delays
- missed deadlines
- anniversary triggers before the date arrives
- kids acting younger than their age
Do:
- rotate the recovery lead if possible
- use a weekly claims review, not constant claims talk
- ask for creditor hardship before default
- keep one meal routine
- protect sleep
- resume one normal activity
- make a written plan for the anniversary
- ask for help with forms, not vague "support"
Ask people for concrete tasks:
- sit with me while I call FEMA
- take the kids for two hours
- scan these receipts
- drive me to the DMV
- help compare bids
- bring dinner Tuesday
This is not weakness.
This is workload plus grief.

## Year 1+: Reconstruction
Reconstruction does not mean everything is fixed.
It means the disaster becomes part of the household story without controlling every hour.
Common signs:
- more acceptance
- new routines
- grief waves
- renewed anger at delays
- less constant adrenaline
- clearer decisions
- anniversary reactions
- changed risk tolerance
- changed friendships
The one-year mark can hit hard.
Plan it.
Do not let it ambush the household.
Options:
- visit the rebuilt site
- avoid the site
- hold a simple meal
- thank helpers
- memorialize a pet or person lost
- finish one practical task
- spend the day away
- schedule therapy that week
- tell school or work the date may be hard
Do not compare grief speed.
Different people in the same home will recover at different rates.

## Warning signs in adults
Seek help fast for:
- suicidal thoughts
- suicide plan
- giving away possessions
- talking about being a burden
- heavy alcohol or drug use
- escalating rage
- threats toward others
- emotional numbness that blocks basic functioning
- panic attacks that stop normal tasks
- nightmares or flashbacks lasting more than 30 days
- inability to sleep for multiple nights
- stopping essential medications
- hearing or seeing things others do not
- domestic violence
- inability to care for children, elders, or self
Call 988 for crisis.
Call 911 if someone is in immediate danger.
Move firearms and excess medications out of reach during crisis.
That single act can save a life.

## Warning signs in kids
Children often show distress through behavior.
Watch for:
- bedwetting after being dry
- clinginess
- nightmares
- school refusal
- new fears
- stomachaches or headaches
- aggression
- withdrawal
- loss of play
- reenacting the disaster
- fear when it rains, winds, shakes, or sirens sound
- asking the same safety question repeatedly
- trying to act like the parent
Do:
- keep routines simple
- tell the truth in age-appropriate words
- say what adults are doing today to keep them safe
- let them help with small tasks
- keep bedtime predictable
- warn teachers and counselors
- limit graphic media
Do not:
- over-promise
- make them the messenger between adults
- hide all facts, then expect trust
- force repeated retelling
- punish regression as defiance

## When to seek professional help
Seek professional help when any of these are true:
- symptoms persist more than one month
- distress blocks work, school, parenting, sleep, or basic care
- safety concern exists
- substance use is increasing
- domestic conflict is escalating
- a child is regressing or refusing school
- grief is stuck in daily impairment
A trauma therapist helps by:
- reducing nightmares, avoidance, panic, and intrusive memories
- teaching body-based calming skills
- helping the brain file the event as past, not present
- using methods such as trauma-focused CBT, EMDR, or exposure-based care when appropriate
A family therapist helps by:
- stopping blame cycles
- making decision rules
- helping partners hear different coping styles
- setting boundaries with relatives
- protecting kids from adult conflict
A psychiatric prescriber helps by:
- restarting interrupted medications
- treating severe insomnia, depression, anxiety, PTSD symptoms, mania, or psychosis
- preventing dangerous withdrawal from benzodiazepines, alcohol, or other substances
- deciding when medication is short-term support versus long-term treatment
A school counselor helps by:
- watching behavior during the day
- arranging supports
- helping with school refusal
- connecting the family to district resources

Suicide and Crisis Lifeline:

Also use:
- local community mental health clinic
- county behavioral health department
- tribal health services
- faith community if trusted
- employee assistance program
- school counselor
- pediatrician
- primary-care clinician
- telehealth therapy
- peer support groups
Do not wait for perfect care.
Start with the reachable care.

## 5 most important actions

1. Protect sleep, meals, medication, and daylight in the first week.
2. Expect anger and fatigue when the disillusionment phase begins.
3. Call 1-800-985-5990 for disaster distress before isolation deepens.
4. Use 988 or emergency services for suicidal thoughts, threats, or immediate danger.
5. Plan the one-year anniversary before it arrives.

## Common mistakes

- Treating week-one adrenaline as proof the household has recovered.
- Letting claims talk fill every meal and every bedtime.
- Ignoring nightmares, rage, or numbness because there is still paperwork to do.
- Telling children "everything is fine" when they can see it is not.
- Using alcohol as the daily off-switch.
- Waiting for collapse before asking for specific help.

## Before it happens, do this once
Write local mental-health crisis numbers, pediatrician numbers, prescriber numbers, and trusted helper names on the household roster before a disaster.
